## Abstract

Epistemic hedging — words like "probably," "certainly," and "possibly" — has been psychometrically calibrated since the 1960s, with median probabilities for verbal expressions remarkably stable across more than fifty years of replication. We investigate whether this calibration is recoverable as a **verbal probability axis** in frozen pretrained pooled sentence embeddings. Within syntactic types, verbal probability is the dominant linear direction across five architecturally diverse embedding models (supervised Spearman $\rho > 0.90$), and the axis triangulates against two independent psychometric datasets at $\rho \approx 0.97$ in both. The same axis transfers in ranking across eight typologically diverse languages via a multilingual encoder, is robust to an order of magnitude of dimensional compression on a Matryoshka-trained model, and passes permutation and label-shuffling null tests on the three larger-$n$ syntactic types. Rank-1 representation-level erasure of the axis clears two complementary nulls — a cosine-matched random-direction control and a label-permutation null — at $p = 0.002$ on the headline cross-type pair across two architectures, the embedding-class structural analogue of activation-steering experiments standard in the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature. We frame the empirical convergence across three psychometric datasets, six embedding architectures, and eight languages as evidence that distributional embedding geometry and human probability semantics are convergent measurements of one underlying epistemic dimension.

## 1. Introduction

\citet{mikolov2013linguistic} demonstrated that semantic relationships manifest as consistent vector directions in word embedding space — *king − man + woman ≈ queen*. The Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH) generalizes this observation: many human-meaningful concepts correspond to linear directions in language model representations \citep{park2024linear}. Three lines of recent work bear on whether epistemic content fits this pattern.

**Linear features in decoder LLM internal states.** \citet{marks2023geometry} identify a truth direction in residual stream activations. \citet{burger2024truth} refine truth representations to handle polarity confounds. \citet{yu2025directions} generalize one-dimensional truth directions to multi-dimensional cones. \citet{ji2025calibrating} identify a single linear "verbal uncertainty" feature in residual streams that can be causally manipulated to reduce overconfident hallucinations. \citet{lepori2026fantasy} link decoder hidden-state directions to graded human plausibility and modal judgments. \citet{valois2025frame} extend single-token concept directions to multi-token "frames" via the Frame Representation Hypothesis, with steering experiments on the unembedding matrix. All of these works share a methodological commitment: they study internal states of decoder LLMs — residual streams, unembedding matrices, layer-wise activations — where token-level causal intervention (steering, activation patching) is natural because the model generates tokens.

**Verbal-probability psychometrics elicited behaviorally from language models.** A parallel literature studies how language models interpret hedge expressions like "likely," "probably," and "possibly" by *prompting* the model and comparing its outputs to human numerical judgments. \citet{sileo2023probing} probe pretrained models for words-of-estimative-probability classification (with fine-tuning). \citet{belem2024perceptions} compare LLM and human numerical interpretations of linguistic uncertainty expressions through prompted-output protocols. \citet{tang2026evaluation} extend this to multilingual estimative uncertainty. \citet{wang2024calibrating} calibrate certainty-phrase distributions using transport-based methods. These papers establish that verbal probability semantics are recoverable from language models *behaviorally*, but they do not extract a geometric structure from frozen embedding representations.

**Pretrained sentence embedding probing for human-aligned graded properties.** A third line studies pooled sentence representations directly. Most directly relevant: \citet{jacobs2022masked} probe mean-pooled RoBERTa sentence embeddings for human cloze-based uncertainty (next-word predictability and sentential constraint), demonstrating that pooled embedding spaces encode some form of human-aligned uncertainty. \citet{chen2019uncertain} show that fine-tuned BERT-based regressors can predict continuous human probability judgments in sentence-pair NLI. \citet{schuster2019harnessing} predict human scalar pragmatic ratings from sentence encoders. \citet{pei2021measuring} regress continuous certainty in scientific writing. These works establish that pooled sentence embeddings carry *some* graded epistemic content — but for cloze probability, NLI inference strength, or rhetorical certainty rather than verbal probability semantics, and not as a frozen-geometry linear axis calibrated against psychometric data.

**The gap.** No prior work has demonstrated a *continuously calibrated verbal probability axis* in *frozen pretrained pooled sentence embeddings*, *aligned with human psychometric judgments* and *validated against independent psychometric datasets*. We address this gap. We use the \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} dataset, which provides empirically calibrated probability distributions for 53 verbal expressions (e.g., "likely" → median 71.1%, "possible" → median 38.5%) collected from 238 science writers. We embed these in controlled sentence templates, extract difference vectors from bare claims, and test whether the resulting geometry encodes a calibrated probability axis. We further test cross-validation against two independent psychometric datasets \citep{vogel2022systematic,wintle2019verbal}, zero-shot ranking transfer to eight typologically diverse languages, robustness to dimensional truncation, null-hypothesis controls, and **concept erasure as functional validation** — the embedding-class analogue of the causal-intervention experiments standard in the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature.

**Our contributions:**

1. **A calibrated within-type verbal probability axis triangulated across three psychometric datasets.** Five architecturally diverse embedding models recover a single linear direction within syntactic types (Spearman $\rho > 0.90$ supervised, 0.59–0.94 leave-one-out on a Mosteller-derived analysis set partitioned across four syntactic types; §3.1). The axis cross-validates against two independent psychometric datasets spanning over fifty years of replication: Mosteller$\to$\citet{vogel2022systematic} $\rho = 0.991$, Mosteller$\to$\citet{wintle2019verbal} $\rho = 0.967$ ($n \approx 924$). Convergence across datasets collected by independent groups on different populations argues against an artifact of any single calibration source. To our knowledge this is the first demonstration of such structure in frozen pretrained pooled sentence embeddings.

2. **An embedding-class structural analogue of decoder-LLM causal intervention.** Rank-1 representation-level concept erasure of the probability axis from cross-type embeddings raises calibration MAE by 17–25 percentage points on the predicative$\leftrightarrow$modal pair, with the headline pair clearing two complementary nulls — a cosine-matched random-direction control (testing geometric correlation) and a label-permutation null on the eraser axis's training labels (testing probability content) — at $p = 0.002$ in all four model $\times$ direction combinations across two architectures. Cross-pair Pearson $r(\cos, \Delta\mathrm{MAE}) = 0.92 / 0.86$ across all twelve ordered cross-type pairs is the structural signature confirming that downstream linear decoders use the probability content the axis carries, not merely correlated embedding geometry.

3. **Cross-architecture and cross-linguistic robustness, with informative negatives.** The finding holds across six pretrained embedding models spanning five architectural families (BERT, BERT-large, MoE, decoder-derived embedding heads, multilingual XLM-R-based) and ranks hedge expressions zero-shot in eight typologically diverse languages via a multilingual encoder (mean ranking $|\rho| = 0.93$). Permutation null tests on the three larger-$n$ syntactic types yield $p \leq 0.005$, and a grammatically clean evaluative non-epistemic control returns LOO $\rho = -0.41$ versus 0.87 for real hedges. Three negative results sharpen the picture: interpretive precision (Mosteller IQR width) is not in the embedding geometry; no ensemble of within-type axes beats the single modal axis on novel-phrase generalization; and negation breaks linear composition in the geometrically appropriate manner of a probability-reflecting operation.

## 2. Related Work

**Linear features in decoder LLM internal activations.** The Linear Representation Hypothesis \citep{park2024linear} has been formalized and tested most extensively on decoder LLM internal states. \citet{marks2023geometry} identify a truth direction in residual streams; \citet{burger2024truth} refine it for polarity; \citet{yu2025directions} generalize one-dimensional truth directions to multi-dimensional cones. The two most direct framing-level comparators are \citet{ji2025calibrating}, who locate a Verbal Uncertainty Feature in residual streams of three 7B-class LLMs via a difference-in-means construction with LLM-as-a-Judge labels and validate it through inference-time activation steering, and \citet{lepori2026fantasy}, who extract modal difference vectors from late-layer hidden states of four model families that linearly discriminate four expert-labeled modal categories with preliminary causal-steering evidence. \citet{valois2025frame} extend the LRH from single tokens to multi-token frames on the unembedding matrix. All of these share two methodological commitments: they operate on decoder LLM internal activations, and they validate functional content via token-level causal intervention — naturally available because the model generates tokens.

**Verbal-probability semantics elicited behaviorally.** A parallel literature studies how language models interpret hedge expressions through prompting rather than representation \citep{sileo2023probing,belem2024perceptions,tang2026evaluation,wang2024calibrating}. These works locate verbal probability semantics in model outputs, not geometrically in representations.

**Pooled sentence representation probing.** A third, smaller line probes pooled or encoded sentence representations directly. The closest precursor in our model class is \citet{jacobs2022masked}, who use mean-pooled RoBERTa with the critical word masked to predict cloze probability and entropy through trained per-item classifiers; their target is sentential constraint via next-word predictability and their method is per-item binary classifiers correlated against external signals, where ours is direct ridge regression of pooled-embedding differences onto psychometric medians for the explicit hedge lexicon. \citet{chen2019uncertain} fine-tune BERT for continuous NLI entailment probability; \citet{schuster2019harnessing}, \citet{pei2021measuring}, \citet{lee2015event}, \citet{stanovsky2017integrating}, \citet{bhatia2017vector}, and \citet{shen2023sen2pro} variously locate scalar implicature strength, rhetorical certainty in scientific writing, scalar factuality, distributional event likelihood, or embedding noise — none extract a frozen-geometry verbal-probability axis cross-validated against independent psychometric datasets.

**The gap.** Across these three lines, no prior work demonstrates the conjunction this paper fills: a *continuously calibrated* verbal-probability axis (not categorical classification or trained-classifier outputs), in *frozen pretrained pooled sentence embeddings* (not decoder LLM internal activations or fine-tuned task-specific models), *aligned with human psychometric judgments* (not LLM-as-a-Judge or expert-assigned category labels), and *validated against independent psychometric datasets* spanning over fifty years of replication \citep{mosteller1990quantifying,vogel2022systematic,wintle2019verbal}. The contribution is parallel in spirit to the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature — same family of finding, that a graded epistemic property is recoverable as a linear direction in a pretrained representation — but conducted in a model class where token-level causal intervention is unavailable, where representation-level concept erasure (§4.4) is the appropriate analogue, and where human psychometric data is the appropriate calibration target.

## 3. Method

We extract a verbal probability axis from frozen pretrained pooled sentence embeddings, calibrate it against human psychometric medians, and evaluate it against in-sample fit, leave-one-out generalization, two independent psychometric datasets, eight typologically diverse languages, several baselines, three null-hypothesis controls, and a representation-level concept-erasure protocol. The protocol is identical across experiments: every script in the codebase is self-contained and reuses the same difference-vector construction, ridge-regression objective, and linear calibration step, parameterized only by the syntactic type of the hedge expressions and the embedding model. Math notation: vectors are column vectors, $\|\cdot\|$ is the $\ell_2$ norm, $\hat{v} = v/\|v\|$ denotes the unit-normalized form of $v$, $\rho$ is Spearman rank correlation, and MAE is mean absolute error in percentage points.

### 3.1 Data

**Primary calibration: \citet{mosteller1990quantifying}.** We work from the \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} corpus of 53 verbal probability expressions rated by 238 science writers, supplied as `docs/mosteller_youtz_1990_full.csv` with 25th-percentile, median, 75th-percentile, and inter-quartile-range values on a $[0, 100]$ scale. One row ("Doubtful") lacks all percentile and median fields in the source and is therefore excluded from every analysis; the remaining 52 entries form the **Mosteller-derived analysis set** the paper draws on. We use the median as the target probability throughout; the IQR is reserved for the precision-encoding probe in §4.7.

**Cross-validation datasets.** Two independent psychometric datasets test whether the trained axis generalizes beyond the calibration source. \citet{vogel2022systematic} is a systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 verbal-probability studies spanning 1967–2018, supplied as `docs/vogel_2022_systematic_review.csv`; we extract the median pooled across studies for each expression that matches our syntactic types. \citet{wintle2019verbal} ($n \approx 924$) provides a third independent dataset; we transcribe nine predicative and three frequency-adverbial values from their Figure 2 and Table 1 (hardcoded in `experiment_08_bimodality_wintle.py`). All three sources collect aggregate human probability judgments under non-verifiable contexts following the methodological framework of \citet{wallsten1986measuring} and \citet{budescu1995processing}; the convergence of three datasets collected by different groups in different decades on different populations is the basis for the cross-dataset generalization claim in §4.2.

**Type classification.** Mixing syntactic types confounds the analysis: PCA on the 52 mixed-type Mosteller-derived difference vectors (one sentence per source entry with a median) recovers PC1 as a syntactic-frame direction (28.7% of variance, ordering noun-phrase constructions against frequency adverbs) rather than as the probability axis (FINDINGS-01). Within-type analysis is therefore not a methodological convenience but a load-bearing decision that isolates the semantic signal from the syntactic envelope (§4.1; cf. the polarity confound \citealp{burger2024truth} report for truth directions). We partition the analysis set into four syntactic types, each with a single sentence template that holds syntax constant across all expressions of that type. Let $T_{\text{type}}(x)$ denote the template for syntactic type with hedge expression $x$ substituted for the placeholder, and let $s_0$ denote the bare claim "The experiment will succeed." The four types and their templates are:

- **Predicative adjective** ($n = 13$): $T_{\text{pred}}(x) = $ "It is $x$ that the experiment will succeed."
- **Frequency adverb** ($n = 19$): $T_{\text{freq}}(x) = $ "The experiment will $x$ succeed."
- **Noun phrase** ($n = 11$): $T_{\text{np}}(x) = $ "There is a(n) $x$ that the experiment will succeed."
- **Modal adverb** ($n = 10$): $T_{\text{mod}}(x) = $ "The experiment will $x$ succeed."

The frequency-adverb and modal-adverb templates share a syntactic slot but encode different semantic types (base-rate frequency versus epistemic confidence); the modal axis is not derivable from the frequency axis (§4.1, FINDINGS-03). The modal-adverb items are derived as adverbial forms of corresponding predicative entries (e.g., the modal "certainly" reuses the median of the predicative "Certain"), so the four within-type partitions sum to 53 template-instance items distributed across 43 unique source rows.

Three modal subsets appear in the codebase, and we name them explicitly to keep claims precise. **M10** is the ten Mosteller-grounded adverbial forms (`certainly`, `almost certainly`, `very likely`, `likely`, `probably`, `very probably`, `possibly`, `unlikely`, `very unlikely`, `improbably`); it is the canonical modal axis used in §4.1 (Table 1), §4.4 (concept erasure), and §4.6 (null-hypothesis controls). **M15** is an expanded set of fifteen items — eight of M10 plus seven author-estimated additions (`conceivably`, `definitely`, `perhaps`, `maybe`, `presumably`, `undoubtedly`, `arguably`) — used for sample-size headroom in the §4.5 robustness probes (truncation, compound composition, bimodality) and §4.7 (ensemble baseline). M15 trades calibration grounding for size, and §4.5 prose marks results that depend on it as exploratory rather than calibrated. A third translation-pairing modal subset (10 items, of which 7 are M10-derived and 3 are author-estimated) is used by the §4.3 cross-linguistic experiment for translation parity across languages and is treated similarly. Item-level provenance for each set is recorded in `paper_validation.py` and `experiment_*.py` headers.

Five models are used for the within-type analyses; the sixth (bge-m3) supports the multilingual experiment. We make no fine-tuning, no head training, and no architectural modifications; the qualifier "frozen pretrained" in the novelty claim depends on this.

Cross-architecture robustness (§4.1) is the basis for the claim that the verbal probability axis is a property of pretrained pooled-embedding geometry rather than of any specific architecture or training corpus.

### 3.3 Axis extraction

**Difference-vector construction.** For each syntactic type $T$ with expressions $\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ and Mosteller medians $\{y_1, \ldots, y_n\}$, we embed the bare claim $s_0$ and each templated sentence $T(x_i)$ using the model's pooled output, yielding $\mathbf{e}_0$ and $\{\mathbf{e}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{e}_n\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$. The input feature for expression $x_i$ is the difference vector
$$
\mathbf{d}_i \;=\; \mathbf{e}_i - \mathbf{e}_0,
$$
which subtracts off the propositional content shared across all expressions in the type and isolates the contribution of the hedge phrase. Differencing against a shared bare claim is the same construction \citet{mikolov2013linguistic} used to expose word-level analogies and that the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature uses to extract truth and uncertainty directions from residual streams \citep{marks2023geometry,ji2025calibrating}.

**Ridge regression on Mosteller medians.** We standardize the medians $\tilde{y}_i = (y_i - \bar{y})/\mathrm{std}(y)$ and stack the difference vectors as rows of $D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$. The probability axis is the unit-normalized solution to a ridge regression \citep{hoerl1970ridge} with regularization $\lambda = 0.1$:
$$
\mathbf{w}^{*} \;=\; \bigl(D^{\top} D + \lambda I_d\bigr)^{-1} D^{\top} \tilde{\mathbf{y}}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{w}} \;=\; \mathbf{w}^{*} / \|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|.
$$
Solving the linear system in $d$ dimensions on $n \le 19$ samples is severely under-determined; the ridge term selects the minimum-norm solution along the dominant probability-correlated direction in the column span of $D$ and stabilizes against numerical conditioning issues at the high embedding dimensions ($d \in \{768, 1024, 4096\}$). The choice $\lambda = 0.1$ is fixed across all experiments and all models for cross-cell comparability rather than per-cell optimum: a four-decade sweep on $\lambda \in \{0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10\}$ across nomic-embed-text v1.5 and mxbai-embed-large by all four within-type axes (eight cells) confirms that LOO Spearman $\rho$ remains within 0.072 of the $\lambda = 0.1$ reference everywhere, with the worst-case drop on nomic predicative ($\rho = 0.874$ at $\lambda = 0.1$ falling to $0.802$ at $\lambda = 10$). The sweep is asymmetric, however: on six of the eight (model $\times$ type) cells a smaller $\lambda$ would yield a higher LOO $\rho$ than $\lambda = 0.1$, and the modal axis is the cell where regularizer choice does the most rank shuffling — most strikingly mxbai modal, where the reference $\lambda = 0.1$ sits at the sweep minimum (LOO $\rho = 0.806$, vs. $0.903$ at $\lambda = 0.001$ and $0.869$ at $\lambda = 10$), consistent with the small-$n$ rank-saturation regime developed in §6 (L4).

**Linear calibration.** The unit axis $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ ranks expressions correctly but does not return probabilities on the percentage scale humans use. We recover the calibration via ordinary least squares on the in-sample projections $p_i = \mathbf{d}_i^{\top} \hat{\mathbf{w}}$ against the unstandardized Mosteller medians $y_i$:
$$
\hat{y}_i \;=\; \alpha \cdot \mathbf{d}_i^{\top} \hat{\mathbf{w}} + \beta, \qquad \text{with } (\alpha, \beta) \text{ from } \mathrm{OLS}\bigl(\{p_i\}, \{y_i\}\bigr).
$$
Predicted probabilities are clipped to $[0, 100]$. The calibration is a one-dimensional affine map; it cannot improve ranking over the unit projection but is what allows continuous predictions on the same percentage scale used in psychometric reporting, and is what the abstract's "continuously calibrated" qualifier refers to. We train one $(\hat{\mathbf{w}}, \alpha, \beta)$ triple per (model, syntactic type) pair, yielding twenty axes for the within-type analyses (five models × four types) plus the eight cross-linguistic axes (bge-m3 × eight languages × {predicative, modal}). Sentence templates use a single propositional content ("the experiment will succeed") for the load-bearing analyses; FINDINGS-01 reports cross-content validation showing axis ranking $\rho > 0.69$ on alternative bare claims ("the treatment will be effective", "the prediction will be correct").

### 3.4 Evaluation strands

We evaluate the trained axis along six strands. (1) **In-sample fit and leave-one-out generalization** with bootstrap 95% CIs over 10,000 resamples, retraining $(\hat{\mathbf{w}}, \alpha, \beta)$ on the $n-1$ remaining items per type (§4.1). (2) **Cross-dataset evaluation**, applying the Mosteller-trained axis without retraining to \citet{vogel2022systematic} and \citet{wintle2019verbal}; we treat the cross-dataset correlations as population-level psychometric replication rather than out-of-sample lexical novelty tests, since expression overlap between training and test sources is not filtered (§4.2). (3) **Unsupervised baselines** — random direction, mean-difference centroid \citep{marks2023geometry,ji2025calibrating}, and PCA PC1, all routed through the same calibration step (§4.1). (4) **Null-hypothesis controls** — Mosteller-label permutation, an evaluative non-epistemic adjective control, and a Uniform$(0, 100)$ random-label control, per type (§4.6). (5) **Cross-linguistic transfer**, applying the English-trained axis without retraining to translated hedge expressions in eight typologically diverse languages via bge-m3, with Spearman $\rho$ as the load-bearing ranking-transfer metric and MAE reported only as a translation-pairing diagnostic (§4.3, §6 L2). (6) **Concept erasure**, rank-1 projection of axis $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A$ from type-$B$ difference vectors via $\mathbf{d}_i' = \mathbf{d}_i - (\mathbf{d}_i^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A)\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A$ followed by re-decoding with type $B$'s calibrated triple, evaluated against two complementary nulls (cosine-matched random direction; label-permutation on the eraser axis's training labels; §4.4).

## 4. Results

### 4.1 The syntactic confound and within-type probability axes

When verbal probability expressions are pooled across the four syntactic types of §3.1, the dominant linear direction in the embedding space is *not* probability but syntax. PCA on the 52 mixed-type difference vectors $\{\mathbf{d}_i\}$ from nomic-embed-text v1.5 returns a first principal component that explains 28.7% of the variance, and inspection of its extremes orders constructions by template family rather than by Mosteller median: noun-phrase formulations such as "high probability," "very high probability," and "moderate probability" sit at one end, frequency adverbs such as "very infrequent," "not often," and "not very often" at the other. The probability signal appears only on PC2 (14.8% of variance, $\rho = -0.68$ against the medians, $p = 2.4 \times 10^{-8}$), and PC1 itself correlates only weakly with the medians ($\rho = -0.28$, $p = 0.05$). The *largest* axis of variation in the cross-type difference-vector cloud is therefore a syntactic-frame direction onto which probability projects only as a secondary signal. This is the structural counterpart in pretrained pooled sentence embeddings of the polarity confound \citet{burger2024truth} report for truth directions in decoder LLM residual streams: a candidate semantic direction recovered without controlling for a co-varying syntactic property is contaminated by that property.

Holding the syntactic type fixed reverses this picture. Within each of the four type partitions, a single ridge-regression axis $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ trained on Mosteller medians by the protocol of §3.3 recovers the probability ordering with high in-sample fidelity and generalizes through leave-one-out (LOO) cross-validation. Table 1 reports both metrics across the five within-type embedding models. In-sample Spearman $\rho$ exceeds 0.90 for every (model, type) cell — the supervised claim foregrounded in the abstract — and LOO $\rho$ falls in the range 0.59–0.94, with embeddinggemma's predicative axis reaching the highest in-sample fit (0.99) and nomic-embed-text v1.5's noun-phrase axis reaching the highest LOO ($\rho = 0.94$). The two LOO cells below 0.65 (embeddinggemma modal at 0.59 and nomic-embed-text v1.5 modal at 0.65) are the small-sample saturation regime $\rho$-based metrics enter at $n = 10$ training items; we discuss this and its implications for the §4.4 concept-erasure analysis in §6 (L7). The dominance of probability on the within-type difference-vector cloud is also visible without supervision: PCA PC1 within type reaches $\rho = 0.98$ against the Mosteller medians on noun phrases for nomic-embed-text v1.5 (PC1 explains 36% of the within-type variance), $\rho = 0.93$ for predicative on mxbai-embed-large (PC1 = 60%), and $\rho = 0.96$ for noun phrase on qwen3-embedding (PC1 = 61%). ### 4.2 Three-dataset psychometric triangulation

The \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} calibration is sixty years old and has been replicated repeatedly under different protocols and on different populations. We test whether the within-type axes trained on Mosteller medians transfer, *without retraining*, to two independently collected psychometric corpora: the \citet{vogel2022systematic} systematic review and meta-analysis aggregating 21 verbal-probability studies from 1967 to 2018, and the \citet{wintle2019verbal} experiment ($n \approx 924$) drawing on a different population with a different elicitation protocol. The Mosteller-trained axis $(\hat{\mathbf{w}}, \alpha, \beta)$ is applied as in §3.4 to surface-form-matched expressions embedded in the same syntactic templates; we report Spearman $\rho$ and MAE on the Vogel and Wintle medians.

For predicative expressions — the type with the largest cross-corpus overlap ($n_{\text{test}} = 11$ for Vogel, 9 for Wintle) — Mosteller-to-Vogel correlations exceed 0.89 across all five models and reach 0.991 on mxbai-embed-large with MAE = 5.1 percentage points (Table 2). Mosteller-to-Wintle predicative results are comparable: $\rho = 0.967$ on mxbai-embed-large (MAE = 3.6%) and $\rho \geq 0.917$ on nomic-embed-text v1.5 and qwen3-embedding (MAE between 3.5% and 6.9%). The frequency-adverb cross-validation is more limited by overlap ($n_{\text{test}} = 3$ on both Vogel and Wintle), but Vogel returns $\rho = 1.000$ on three of the five models with MAEs between 2.4% and 6.7%, and Wintle returns $\rho = 1.000$ on every tested model with MAEs of 4.0–10.5%. Noun-phrase expressions have no cross-corpus matches and are not included in the table; the cross-corpus tests therefore probe predicative and frequency-adverbial calibration directly.

### 4.4 Functional validation via concept erasure

The §3.4 protocol — rank-1 erasure of axis $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A$ from type-$B$ difference vectors followed by re-decoding with type $B$'s calibrated triple — applied across all twelve ordered pairs $(A, B)$ with $A \neq B$ and $A, B \in \{\text{predicative}, \text{frequency adverb}, \text{noun phrase}, \text{modal}\}$ on mxbai-embed-large (1024d) and qwen3-embedding (4096d), yields a single cross-model structural signature. The Pearson correlation between inter-axis cosine alignment $\cos(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A, \hat{\mathbf{w}}_B)$ and the increase in calibrated mean absolute prediction error after erasure ($\Delta\mathrm{MAE}$) is $r = 0.92$ on mxbai-embed-large and $r = 0.86$ on qwen3-embedding across all twelve ordered pairs (Figure 13). Erasing a probability-aligned axis from cross-type embeddings degrades the downstream linear decoder in proportion to the probability content the eraser shares with the target, and this scaling holds across two architecturally distinct models — a 1024-dimensional BERT-large variant and a 4096-dimensional decoder-derived embedding head. We treat cross-model agreement on this cross-pair signature, rather than per-pair functional verdicts, as the load-bearing finding: the four within-type axes carry probability information that downstream decoders use, and the use is graded by geometric alignment in the way one would predict if the four axes were partially redundant projections of a shared epistemic dimension (§5).

The predicative–modal pair, calibrated to the same Mosteller medians in adjective and adverb form respectively, has the strongest a priori reason to share content; its cosine alignment is the highest of the twelve ordered pairs in both models ($\cos = 0.88$ on mxbai-embed-large, $0.79$ on qwen3-embedding). Erasing the predicative axis from modal-type embeddings raises MAE from 6.8 to 25.2 percentage points on mxbai ($\Delta\mathrm{MAE} = +18.4$) and from 5.2 to 22.5 on qwen3 ($+17.4$); the reverse direction (erase modal, decode with predicative) raises MAE from 4.6 to 29.2 on mxbai ($+24.6$) and from 5.5 to 26.0 on qwen3 ($+20.5$). We test the headline pair against two complementary nulls that answer different methodological questions. The cosine-matched random-direction null asks whether this *direction* damages more than a random direction with the same geometric overlap with $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_B$; the label-permutation null asks whether the *probability content* of $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A$ is doing the work, retraining the eraser on shuffled Mosteller medians while holding the template subspace, regularizer, and diff-vector geometry fixed. The headline pair clears both. Real-axis $\Delta\mathrm{MAE}$ exceeds matched-random direction erasure with ratios of $1.02$, $1.12$, $1.17$, and $1.23$ across the four model $\times$ direction combinations (the matched-random control is by construction stringent at high cosine — at $\cos = 0.88$ it reproduces approximately 98\% of the real-axis MAE damage by geometric overlap alone, §6 L3 — which compresses headroom but not interpretability of the ratio). Against the label-permutation null, whose null distribution has substantial variance ($\sigma$ of $1.7$ to $4.3$ percentage points on the headline pair across the four combinations), real $\Delta\mathrm{MAE}$ exceeds every one of 500 shuffles, $p = 0.002$ in all four cases (mxbai Pred$\to$Modal real $+18.37$ vs.\ null $+1.22 \pm 1.71$; mxbai Modal$\to$Pred real $+24.62$ vs.\ $+3.27 \pm 4.28$; qwen3 Pred$\to$Modal real $+17.36$ vs.\ $+1.28 \pm 1.87$; qwen3 Modal$\to$Pred real $+20.49$ vs.\ $+2.67 \pm 3.88$). The label-permutation null compensates directly for the matched-random control's compressed headroom at high cosine: its wider null variance is what tests of probability content rather than geometric overlap should produce, and the headline pair clears both.

We treat MAE rather than $\rho$ as the load-bearing metric throughout §4.4 because in-sample $\rho$ already reaches $\geq 0.95$ across all four within-type axes in both models, leaving rank-based metrics with no headroom to register erasure effects: rank-1 projection compresses magnitudes while preserving order, and Spearman $\rho$ is insensitive to that compression (§6 L4). The $\rho$-only view of the same twelve ordered pairs returns five functional pairs on mxbai and three on qwen3 with zero overlap; the MAE view recovers cross-model agreement, with $r(\cos, \Delta\mathrm{MAE}) = +0.92$ and $+0.86$ contrasting with $r(\cos, \Delta\rho) = -0.72$ and $+0.24$. The cross-pair pattern under the two nulls is the structural observation that mirrors the cross-model $r$ signature. On qwen3-embedding, eleven of twelve ordered pairs clear both nulls simultaneously, with the remaining pair (Modal$\to$frequency adverb) functional under matched-random and trend-level under label-permutation; on mxbai-embed-large, five of twelve pairs clear both nulls, four are functional under matched-random only, and three are functional under neither. Critically, zero of twelve pairs in either model are functional only under the label-permutation null: it is the more stringent test, and where the two nulls disagree, label-permutation is the more conservative direction. We read this as evidence that the matched-random control's high-cosine over-conservatism (§6 L3) and rank-saturation at small $n$ (§6 L4) jointly bound per-pair resolution on individual low-cosine pairs, while the cross-pair structural signal — the $r \approx 0.9$ scaling and the headline pair's clearance of both nulls in both models — is robust. The §4.4 result is the embedding-class structural analogue of the activation-steering and representation-patching protocols standard in the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature \citep{ji2025calibrating,marks2023geometry,valois2025frame,lepori2026fantasy}: rank-1 representation-level erasure under a calibration-aware downstream linear decoder plays the role causal intervention plays in token-generating models, with the methodological framing developed in §5 and the necessary "analogue rather than substitute" caveat in §6 L3. The result reproduces under leave-one-out target decoding (`experiment_11_loo.py`) — for each held-out type-$B$ item, the calibrated triple $(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_B, \alpha_B, \beta_B)$ is refit on the remaining $n_B - 1$ items before applying erasure — with the cross-pair $r(\cos, \Delta\mathrm{MAE})$ in fact rising to $+0.98$ on mxbai-embed-large and $+0.89$ on qwen3-embedding under LOO, and the predicative $\leftrightarrow$ modal headline pair clearing the matched-random LOO null in all four model $\times$ direction combinations ($z_{\text{match\_MAE}}$ from $+3.7$ to $+12.8$).

### 4.5 Robustness: dimensional truncation, novel-phrase generalization, compound composition, bimodality probe

Four robustness probes test the verbal probability axis along axes orthogonal to the Mosteller-Vogel-Wintle calibration: dimensional truncation, novel-phrase generalization, compound-hedge composition, and the bimodal-modifier "possible" probe. Each is consistent with the headline within-type and cross-dataset results of §4.1–§4.2 and constrains the interpretation of those results in a different direction.

**Matryoshka dimensional truncation.** Following the Matryoshka Representation Learning protocol \citep{kusupati2022matryoshka}, we re-extract the within-type axes after truncating each model's pooled embedding to 512, 256, 128, 64, and 32 dimensions and recomputing the difference vectors and ridge axis at each truncated dimensionality. On nomic-embed-text v1.5 (768d, MRL-trained), worst-case LOO $\rho$ across the four within-type axes drops by only 0.027 at 64 dimensions — a 12$\times$ compression that preserves rank-based recovery of the probability axis on every type. Even at 32 dimensions (24$\times$ compression), the worst-case LOO $\rho$ drop remains 0.116, with the modal axis actually *improving* slightly (0.948 $\to$ 0.960). On mxbai-embed-large (1024d, not MRL-trained), the axis is robust to 128 dimensions (8$\times$) but degrades at 64 dimensions (worst-case LOO $\rho$ drop = 0.154), as expected for a model whose loss objective does not enforce nested-prefix sufficiency. Axis-stability cosines between the full-dimensional axis and its truncated counterpart confirm this dichotomy: nomic at 64 dimensions retains cos $\geq 0.94$ to its full-dimensional axis on every type, mxbai cos $\geq 0.92$ at 128 but falls to 0.85–0.93 at 32. Figure 5 plots the full LOO $\rho$ versus dimensionality curve. The MRL-versus-non-MRL contrast is the methodological observation: where a model has been explicitly trained for nested-prefix generality, the verbal probability axis inherits that property without retraining.

![Matryoshka dimensional truncation: LOO $\rho$ versus truncated embedding dimension on nomic-embed-text v1.5 (MRL-trained, 768d) and mxbai-embed-large (non-MRL, 1024d). The MRL-trained model preserves the axis to 64 dimensions (12$\times$ compression); the non-MRL model holds at 128 dimensions.](figures/fig5_truncation.pdf){#fig:truncation}

**Novel-phrase generalization.** The Mosteller calibration covers 53 expressions, none of them informal first-person hedges ("I think," "I believe"), doubt expressions ("I doubt," "I wonder if"), anti-hedges ("obviously," "clearly," "without question"), or constructions outside the survey's vocabulary ("signs point to," "I'd wager," "it stands to reason"). To test whether the trained axes encode probability semantics or only Mosteller-vocabulary lookup, we apply each model's trained axes to a held-out set of 26 such phrases embedded as natural sentences and correlate against author-rated intuition probabilities. Spearman $\rho$ across the 26 phrases reaches 0.752 on nomic-embed-text v1.5, 0.842 on mxbai-embed-large, 0.831 on nomic v2 MoE, 0.870 on embeddinggemma, and 0.895 on qwen3-embedding; on the ten purely novel phrases (the "novel" subset, excluding the informal-hedge and anti-hedge categories), the same correlations rise to 0.796–0.942. The geometry orders informal first-person hedges intuitively (believe > think > imagine > suspect > wonder > doubt > not sure) and correctly assigns sub-50% probabilities to doubt expressions and high-50s/60s to anti-hedges, with a systematic compression effect — natural sentence constructions distribute the difference vector across more dimensions than the controlled templates do, attenuating projection magnitudes toward the center while preserving rank order. The author-rated ground truth limits the strength of the calibration claim; the result is informative as evidence that the axis ranks out-of-vocabulary phrases sensibly, not as a calibrated psychometric mapping for those phrases. We make this distinction explicit and treat the §4.5 numbers as evidence of generalization rather than as calibrated-prediction claims (§6 L1).

**Compound-hedge composition.** Natural language stacks hedge cues — "I think it will probably succeed," "It seems fairly likely" — and the question is whether the difference vector for a compound hedge equals the sum of its component vectors. On qwen3-embedding and mxbai-embed-large, the cosine between a compound difference vector $\mathbf{d}_{A+B}$ and its component sum $\mathbf{d}_A + \mathbf{d}_B$ falls in the range 0.82–0.89 across most compound types (first-person + modal, evidential + modal, attenuator + predicative, double first-person), with a systematic magnitude ratio of approximately 0.75 — the compound is sub-linear in magnitude but near-linear in direction. Negation is the structural exception: $\mathbf{d}_{\neg A}$ is near-orthogonal to $\mathbf{d}_A$ (cos = 0.22 on qwen3-embedding for "not impossible" versus "impossible") rather than antiparallel, and a follow-up probe (FINDINGS-06) finds that negation acts as a *probability-reflecting* operation — negating a high-probability word produces a vector aligned with the negation of other high-probability words ($+0.96$ between "not likely" and "not probable") but anti-correlated with negations of low-probability words ($-0.93$ between "not possible" and "not impossible"). Negation is not a single direction in the embedding, but its action on the verbal probability axis is structured: it inverts the projection. This is the geometrically appropriate behavior for a probability-reflecting axis and constrains how a downstream calibrator should handle negated hedges (Figure 11).

**Bimodal-modifier "possible" probe.** The Mosteller distribution for "possible" is bimodal — one human cluster reads it as logical possibility (5–10%), another as reasonable chance (40–50%) — and the axis, as the IQR result of §4.7 will show, encodes a single point estimate per expression. We probe whether modifiers can disambiguate this in geometry by embedding "{modifier} possible" with twelve modifiers spanning low-leaning ("barely," "just barely," "remotely") to high-leaning ("entirely," "very," "eminently"). On mxbai-embed-large, low-leaning compositions of "possible" project to 0.1–13.4% on the predicative axis and high-leaning compositions to 60.3–74.1%, a spread of 31.6 percentage points (Spearman $\rho = 0.893$ between projection and expected probability across the twelve modifiers). On qwen3-embedding the spread is 29.7 percentage points; on nomic-embed-text v1.5 it is 20.0. The geometry traverses the bimodality range when a disambiguating modifier is present even though it cannot represent the bare-"possible" ambiguity directly. Figure 10 visualizes the spread.

### 4.6 Null-hypothesis controls

Three controls (`experiment_10_null_hypothesis.py`, on nomic-embed-text v1.5) establish that the within-type LOO recovery of §4.1 is in the phrase-to-probability mapping rather than in the protocol or template structure.

**Permutation test.** Randomly permuting the Mosteller medians among the expressions of each type and rerunning the ridge-plus-LOO pipeline 1{,}000 times yields a permuted-LOO-$\rho$ distribution centered near zero with standard deviation 0.29–0.42 per type. For the predicative, frequency-adverb, and noun-phrase axes the real-label LOO $\rho$ clears the 99th percentile of the permuted distribution: predicative $\rho = 0.874$ at $p = 0.003$, frequency adverb $\rho = 0.923$ at $p < 0.001$, noun phrase $\rho = 0.936$ at $p = 0.005$. The same three axes pass on three additional architectures (embeddinggemma, nomic v2 MoE, mxbai-embed-large) at $p \leq 0.029$ on all nine remaining (model $\times$ type) cells; the within-type calibration result of §4.1 is not a one-model finding. The modal axis at $n = 10$ instead shows a smooth cross-model gradient that tracks modal LOO $\rho$ — embeddinggemma at LOO $\rho = 0.59$ ($p = 0.22$) and nomic-embed-text v1.5 at $0.65$ ($p = 0.21$) fail; mxbai-embed-large at $0.81$ sits near the threshold ($p = 0.054$); nomic v2 MoE at $0.92$ passes ($p = 0.004$). A multi-$n$ power analysis on nomic-embed-text v1.5 (`experiment_10b`) appends author-estimated modal values to the Mosteller-grounded $n = 10$ set and reruns the test at $n \in \{10, 12, 14, 17\}$: $p$ collapses three orders of magnitude across $n = 10 \to 14$, crossing $p < 0.05$ between $n = 10$ and $n = 12$ (the expanded-set rows are not a calibration claim; they probe sample-size variation only). §6 L4 develops the small-$n$ model-sensitive reading in full; the modal axis is independently validated as functional in §4.4 via the concept-erasure analysis on $n = 10$.

**Evaluative non-epistemic control.** Thirteen *that*-complement adjectives spanning natural valence ("tragic," "regrettable," "unfortunate," through "encouraging," "fortunate," "wonderful"), placed in the same predicative template and trained against deliberately scrambled arbitrary labels (chosen not to track the natural negative-to-positive ordering), return LOO $\rho = -0.41$ — well below the real-epistemic predicative LOO $\rho = 0.874$ and on the opposite side of zero from any null fit. The negative sign is interpretable: ridge regression detects the embedding's real evaluative axis (tragic$\to$wonderful), so the protocol's LOO predictions track natural valence rather than the arbitrary labels we trained on, consistent with the multiple-axis picture §4.1 establishes from mixed-type PCA. A second *mixed* set including epistemic-leaky adjectives ("expected" $\approx$ "probable", "obvious" $\approx$ "certain") returns LOO $\rho = 0.69$, evidence *for* the geometry's epistemic sensitivity rather than against the calibration claim; §6 L1 develops this in full.

**Random-label control.** Drawing labels from Uniform$(0, 100)$ and rerunning the pipeline 1{,}000 times per type matches the real-label LOO $\rho$ in $\leq 6/1000$ trials on the three larger-$n$ axes ($p \leq 0.006$); the modal axis at $n = 10$ matches in 197/1000 ($p = 0.20$), the same small-$n$ low-power signature as the permutation test. The signal in the three larger-$n$ axes is in the phrase-probability mapping the model learned during pretraining, not in structural properties of the regression.

### 4.7 Negative results

Two informative negatives constrain the interpretation of the verbal probability axis: the IQR result tells us what kind of object the axis is, and the ensemble result tells us that the four within-type axes do not stack into a more accurate combined predictor.

**Interpretive precision is not in the geometry.** \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} report the inter-quartile range (IQR) of human probability ratings per expression alongside the median; "possible" has IQR = 42.7 (P25 = 7.5%, P75 = 50.2%, the bimodal distribution probed in §4.5), while "certain" has IQR = 1.1. We test whether the embedding geometry encodes this interpretive precision by computing the cross-claim consistency of each expression's difference vector — its mean pairwise cosine across 15 propositional contents — and correlating against $-$IQR. If the geometry encoded which expressions humans agree about, low-IQR expressions should produce more consistent cross-claim directions. Across four models (mxbai-embed-large, qwen3-embedding, nomic v2 MoE, embeddinggemma) the correlation $\rho(\text{consistency}, -\text{IQR})$ ranges from $-0.20$ to $+0.05$ with $p > 0.5$ in every case. The geometry encodes a single stable meaning per expression, not the spread of human interpretations of that expression. This is informative for what the verbal probability axis *is*: a point estimator on the population probability mapping, not a representation of the variance of that mapping. A practical calibration system that needs interpretive-precision flags must read them from external psychometric data (the Mosteller IQR column) rather than from the axis. The result is also a constraint on the formal-semantics interpretation in §5: the axis is consistent with treating "probably," "possibly," and their kin as continuous-valued operators on a single probability dimension \citep{lassiter2017graded}, not with treating them as distributional objects encoding rater variance.

**No ensemble of within-type axes beats the single modal axis on novel phrases.** §4.1 yields four within-type axes per model (predicative, frequency adverb, noun phrase, modal). One natural follow-up — given that these axes are moderately correlated (cos = 0.41–0.88 within a single model on mxbai; cf. §5) — is to combine them into a single ensemble predictor. We tested ridge-regressed linear ensemble, magnitude-weighted average, max-projection routing, calibrated logistic stacking, and a naïve four-axis mean against the modal axis alone on the novel-phrase set of §4.5. No ensemble strategy beats the modal axis alone: on mxbai-embed-large the modal-only MAE is 9.7%, the best ensemble (linear ridge) reaches 11.4%, and the oracle that knows the ground truth and picks the best axis per phrase reaches 5.9%. The pattern repeats on qwen3-embedding (modal 5.9%, best ensemble 8.3%, oracle 4.3%) and embeddinggemma (modal 5.6%, best ensemble 7.5%, oracle 4.5%). The modal axis is within 1–4 percentage points of oracle on every model and is the practical fallback for arbitrary natural hedge phrasings. The ensemble's failure to add value is interpretable: the four axes are not independent measurements of a shared latent quantity but partially redundant projections of one underlying epistemic dimension whose modal-frame instantiation happens to dominate the syntactic distribution of natural hedging.

---

## 5. Discussion

A useful way to read the empirical content of this paper is as evidence that distributional embedding geometry and human probability semantics are convergent measurements of one underlying quantity. Three independently collected psychometric datasets spanning more than fifty years \citep{mosteller1990quantifying,vogel2022systematic,wintle2019verbal}, six pretrained embedding models with different architectures, and eight typologically diverse languages all agree on the same continuous probability axis. Each instrument is exposed to a different source of measurement noise — Mosteller's 238-rater sample, Vogel's meta-analytic aggregation across 21 studies, Wintle's $n \approx 924$ expert-elicitation protocol, and the architectural and training-data heterogeneity of the embedding models — and their joint agreement on a common ordering and a common calibration is what one would expect if each were a noisy measurement of a single latent quantity. \citet{schockaert2022formal}'s formal framework for embeddings as epistemic states supplies the measurement-theoretic scaffolding under which this reading is well-typed. The §4.7 IQR negative further sharpens the picture: the geometry encodes a single stable point per expression rather than the spread of human interpretations, consistent with treating "probably," "possibly," and their kin as continuous-valued operators on a single probability dimension \citep{lassiter2017graded} and inconsistent with treating them as distributional objects encoding rater variance. The verbal probability axis is, in this sense, a *point estimator* on the population probability mapping — recovering central tendency without recovering dispersion.

## 6. Limitations

We name four limitations the present study does not resolve. Remediation is framed as scope-of-future-work rather than as a flaw in the present claims.

**(L1) Construct validity: template-only evaluation, author-rated novel phrases.** Every Mosteller, Vogel, and Wintle result reported in §4.1–§4.2, the §4.3 cross-linguistic transfer, and the §4.4 concept-erasure experiment uses three controlled syntactic templates ("It is {X} that the experiment will succeed", and analogues for the four within-type axes). External validity to natural text is therefore an inferential step this paper does not make rigorously. Section 4.5 partially mitigates the concern by showing that the trained axes rank novel non-template phrasings ("I think," "I doubt," "signs point to") with $\rho = 0.75$–$0.94$ across five models against author-rated intuition probabilities, and §4.6 shows a clean evaluative non-epistemic control returns LOO $\rho = -0.41$ — the protocol does not trivially fit arbitrary labels on grammatical adjectives in this template. Two follow-ups would close the gaps together: a corpus study with hedged claims drawn from scientific writing, weather forecasts, or IPCC assessment reports — domains where verbal probability and numerical probability are paired by editorial convention — would convert the ranking-preserved claim into a calibration claim on natural text; and a small Prolific re-rating study ($n \geq 10$ annotators per phrase) on the §4.5 novel phrases would convert the author-rated targets into independent psychometric ratings. Both are reserved for follow-up.

**(L2) Cross-linguistic results are ranking transfer, not native-speaker calibration.** The eight-language probability values used in §4.3 are constructed translations of English Mosteller equivalents (e.g., Japanese ありそうな paired with Mosteller's "likely" $\to 71.1\%$) rather than values elicited from native-speaker psychometric surveys in each target language. The ranking correlation we report (mean $|\rho| = 0.928$) is a defensible claim. The MAE figures are not directly comparable across languages because the "expected" value column is itself a translation artifact, and pragmatic-loaded epistemic markers like Japanese まさか carry contextual implicatures a template-driven evaluation cannot capture (§4.3, Table 3, Japanese modal MAE = 18.1\%). Native-speaker psychometric validation in the eight target languages — replicating Mosteller's 1990 protocol with locally recruited raters — is the natural next step and is reserved for follow-up.

**(L3) Non-causal embedding-class intervention.** The §4.4 concept-erasure experiment establishes that the verbal probability axis is functional in the linear-decoder sense — the headline predicative $\leftrightarrow$ modal pair clears two complementary nulls on both tested architectures (a cosine-matched random-direction control and a label-permutation null on the eraser axis's training labels), with cross-pair Pearson $r(\cos, \Delta\mathrm{MAE}) = 0.92/0.86$ as the structural signature. This is the embedding-class analogue of the activation-steering and representation-patching experiments standard in the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature \citep{ji2025calibrating,lepori2026fantasy,marks2023geometry,valois2025frame}; pretrained pooled sentence embedding models lack a token-generation pathway through which to verify that an intervention changes downstream model output, so representation-level erasure validated by a calibrated downstream linear decoder is the appropriate analogue rather than a substitute. The matched-random control is also stringent at high cosine alignment by construction: at the predicative–modal cosine of 0.88, the matched-random direction reproduces approximately 98\% of the real-axis MAE damage geometrically, compressing the headroom for the real-axis effect to clear $z = +2$. The headline pair clears this stringent threshold and the wider-variance label-permutation null in all four model $\times$ direction combinations. Bridging sentence-embedding probes to decoder-LLM behavior — for example, asking whether the verbal-uncertainty feature \citet{ji2025calibrating} localize in residual streams corresponds, under shared input, to the projection of the same sentence onto the pooled-embedding probability axis — is a natural follow-up question this paper opens but does not answer.

**(L4) Small per-type sample sizes saturate $\rho$-based metrics; the modal axis is most exposed.** Within-type axis training uses Mosteller-derived sets of $n = 10$ (modal), 11 (noun phrase), 13 (predicative), and 19 (frequency adverb), and in-sample fit reaches $\rho \geq 0.95$ on every type for both mxbai-embed-large and qwen3-embedding, leaving rank-based metrics with essentially no headroom to register a functional erasure effect. This is why §4.4 leads with MAE rather than $\rho$: the $\rho$-based view of the concept-erasure results yields 5/12 functional pairs on mxbai vs. 3/12 on qwen3 with zero overlap, which reads as cross-model disagreement and dissolves under MAE. The same regime is visible in the §4.6 modal-axis permutation test, where the cross-model gradient and the multi-$n$ power-analysis curve jointly support a small-$n$ + model-sensitive interpretation: the $p < 0.05$ threshold is crossed between $n = 10$ and $n = 12$ when author-estimated modal values are appended to the Mosteller-grounded set (the expanded-set rows probe sample-size variation and are not a calibration claim), and $p$ tracks modal LOO $\rho$ from clear failures at LOO $\rho \approx 0.6$ through a threshold boundary at $0.81$ to a clean pass at $0.92$. We treat $\rho$-saturation at small $n$ as a property of the available calibration data — Mosteller's 53 expressions remain the field standard six decades into this literature — rather than as a flaw in our extraction method. Modal training is restricted to the ten Mosteller-grounded adverbial expressions; an extension to $\sim 20$ expressions via native-speaker psychometric replication would alleviate the saturation concern and would also support the L2 cross-linguistic remediation if conducted in multiple languages.

---

## 7. Conclusion

We have shown that pretrained pooled sentence embedding models encode a continuously calibrated verbal probability axis aligned with human psychometric data: a single linear direction recovered within syntactic types from frozen pooled representations, calibrated against \citet{mosteller1990quantifying}, cross-validated against \citet{vogel2022systematic} and \citet{wintle2019verbal}, transferred zero-shot in ranking to eight typologically diverse languages, robust to dimensional truncation, and confirmed as functional rather than merely correlated by representation-level concept erasure under a cosine-matched random-direction control. The result holds across six pretrained embedding models spanning five architectural families and survives three null-hypothesis controls. It establishes for the pretrained pooled sentence embedding class a phenomenon the recent linear-feature interpretability literature has documented for decoder LLM internal states, with representation-level concept erasure standing in for token-level causal intervention and human psychometric data standing in for model-internal calibration targets.

Four directions extend the present work most directly. The first is native-speaker psychometric replication of the Mosteller protocol in the eight target languages of §4.3, which would convert the cross-linguistic ranking-transfer claim into a calibration-transfer claim and resolve the translation-pairing ambiguity §6 L2 names for languages such as Japanese where pragmatic-loaded epistemic markers diverge from English equivalents. The second is real-text corpus evaluation: hedged claims drawn from scientific writing, weather forecasts, and IPCC assessment reports — domains in which verbal probability and numerical probability are paired by editorial convention — would convert the template-controlled calibration of §4.1–§4.2 into a calibration claim on natural hedged text and address the external-validity concern of §6 L1. The third is bridging pretrained pooled sentence embedding probes to decoder-LLM internal-state probes by asking whether the verbal-uncertainty feature \citet{ji2025calibrating} localize in residual streams corresponds, under shared input, to the projection of the same sentence onto the pooled-embedding probability axis we recover here; \citeauthor{ji2025calibrating}'s Appendix E.2 already establishes a small bridge of this kind, and a systematic comparison would test whether the two model classes converge on the same epistemic dimension in the sense developed in §5 (cf. §6 L3). The fourth is enlarging the underlying psychometric calibration corpus — extending Mosteller's modal subset with native-speaker-validated supplements would alleviate the small-sample saturation §6 L4 imposes on the modal axis. We close on the framing §5 develops: distributional embedding geometry and human psychometric surveys are most usefully read as convergent instruments measuring one underlying quantity, and the present paper is one empirical step toward that frame.
