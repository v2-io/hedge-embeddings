## Abstract

Epistemic hedging — words like "probably," "certainly," and "possibly" — has been psychometrically calibrated since the 1960s, with median probability values for verbal expressions remarkably stable across more than fifty years of replication \citep{mosteller1990quantifying,vogel2022systematic,wintle2019verbal}. We investigate whether this calibration is recoverable as a **verbal probability axis** in frozen pretrained pooled sentence embeddings. Within syntactic types, verbal probability is the dominant linear direction in five architecturally diverse pretrained embedding models (Spearman ρ > 0.90 supervised, 0.59–0.94 leave-one-out on the 53-expression Mosteller dataset). The axis cross-validates against two independent psychometric datasets (Vogel 2022 meta-analysis: ρ = 0.991, MAE = 5.1%; Wintle 2019, n ≈ 924: ρ = 0.967, MAE = 3.6%), transfers zero-shot to eight typologically diverse languages via a multilingual model (mean ranking correlation |ρ| = 0.93), survives 12× dimensional compression on a Matryoshka-trained model (LOO ρ drop = 0.027 at 64 dimensions), and passes permutation null-hypothesis tests on the three larger-n axes (p ≤ 0.005). Rank-1 projection erasure of the axis from cross-type embeddings raises mean absolute prediction error by 18–25 percentage points on the strongest-aligned axis pair (predicative ↔ modal), with degradation correlating with inter-axis cosine alignment at Pearson r = 0.92 (mxbai-embed-large) / 0.86 (qwen3-embedding) across all 12 ordered cross-type pairs. The effect functionally exceeds a cosine-matched random-direction control on both models (MAE z-score > +2 across all four headline pair × model × direction combinations; 9 of 12 ordered pairs functional on both models in the MAE view) — evidence the axis carries probability content beyond what its geometric overlap with the target axis alone would explain. Recent work has documented analogous structure in two separate model contexts: linear features in *decoder LLM* internal states \citep{ji2025calibrating,marks2023geometry}, where token-level causal intervention is natural, and verbal-probability calibrations behaviorally elicited from prompted LLMs \citep{belem2024perceptions,tang2026evaluation}. Pretrained pooled sentence embedding models lack a token-generation pathway, making representation-level intervention the model-class analogue and human psychometric data the appropriate calibration target. The empirical convergence across three psychometric datasets, six embedding models, eight languages, and the functional-erasure result is consistent with distributional embedding geometry and human probability semantics being convergent measurements of one underlying epistemic dimension.

## 1. Introduction

\citet{mikolov2013linguistic} demonstrated that semantic relationships manifest as consistent vector directions in word embedding space — *king − man + woman ≈ queen*. The Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH) generalizes this observation: many human-meaningful concepts correspond to linear directions in language model representations \citep{park2024linear}. Three lines of recent work bear on whether epistemic content fits this pattern.

**Linear features in decoder LLM internal states.** \citet{marks2023geometry} identify a truth direction in residual stream activations. \citet{burger2024truth} refine truth representations to handle polarity confounds. \citet{yu2025directions} generalize one-dimensional truth directions to multi-dimensional cones. \citet{ji2025calibrating} identify a single linear "verbal uncertainty" feature in residual streams that can be causally manipulated to reduce overconfident hallucinations. \citet{lepori2026fantasy} link decoder hidden-state directions to graded human plausibility and modal judgments. \citet{valois2025frame} extend single-token concept directions to multi-token "frames" via the Frame Representation Hypothesis, with steering experiments on the unembedding matrix. All of these works share a methodological commitment: they study internal states of decoder LLMs — residual streams, unembedding matrices, layer-wise activations — where token-level causal intervention (steering, activation patching) is natural because the model generates tokens.

**Verbal-probability psychometrics elicited behaviorally from language models.** A parallel literature studies how language models interpret hedge expressions like "likely," "probably," and "possibly" by *prompting* the model and comparing its outputs to human numerical judgments. \citet{sileo2023probing} probe pretrained models for words-of-estimative-probability classification (with fine-tuning). \citet{belem2024perceptions} compare LLM and human numerical interpretations of linguistic uncertainty expressions through prompted-output protocols. \citet{tang2026evaluation} extend this to multilingual estimative uncertainty. \citet{wang2024calibrating} calibrate certainty-phrase distributions using transport-based methods. These papers establish that verbal probability semantics are recoverable from language models *behaviorally*, but they do not extract a geometric structure from frozen embedding representations.

**Pretrained sentence embedding probing for human-aligned graded properties.** A third line studies pooled sentence representations directly. Most directly relevant: \citet{jacobs2022masked} probe mean-pooled RoBERTa sentence embeddings for human cloze-based uncertainty (next-word predictability and sentential constraint), demonstrating that pooled embedding spaces encode some form of human-aligned uncertainty. \citet{chen2019uncertain} show that fine-tuned BERT-based regressors can predict continuous human probability judgments in sentence-pair NLI. \citet{schuster2019harnessing} predict human scalar pragmatic ratings from sentence encoders. \citet{pei2021measuring} regress continuous certainty in scientific writing. These works establish that pooled sentence embeddings carry *some* graded epistemic content — but for cloze probability, NLI inference strength, or rhetorical certainty rather than verbal probability semantics, and not as a frozen-geometry linear axis calibrated against psychometric data.

**The gap.** No prior work has demonstrated a *continuously calibrated verbal probability axis* in *frozen pretrained pooled sentence embeddings*, *aligned with human psychometric judgments* and *validated against independent psychometric datasets*. We address this gap. We use the \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} dataset, which provides empirically calibrated probability distributions for 53 verbal expressions (e.g., "likely" → median 71.1%, "possible" → median 38.5%) collected from 238 science writers. We embed these in controlled sentence templates, extract difference vectors from bare claims, and test whether the resulting geometry encodes a calibrated probability axis. We further test cross-validation against two independent psychometric datasets \citep{vogel2022systematic,wintle2019verbal}, zero-shot ranking transfer to eight typologically diverse languages, robustness to dimensional truncation, null-hypothesis controls, and **concept erasure as functional validation** — the embedding-class analogue of the causal-intervention experiments standard in the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature.

**Our contributions:**

1. **A continuously calibrated verbal probability axis in frozen pretrained pooled sentence embeddings.** Within syntactic types, verbal probability is the dominant linear direction across five architecturally diverse pretrained embedding models — Spearman ρ > 0.90 supervised, 0.59–0.94 leave-one-out on the \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} dataset. This is continuous calibration, not classification: predicted probabilities track Mosteller medians on the same scale humans use. To our knowledge this is the first demonstration of such structure in this model class — prior work has shown human-aligned uncertainty behavior in prompted LLMs \citep{belem2024perceptions,tang2026evaluation}, linear uncertainty features in decoder activations \citep{ji2025calibrating,lepori2026fantasy}, and pooled-embedding probes for cloze-style uncertainty \citep{jacobs2022masked} and NLI inference strength \citep{chen2019uncertain}, but not a psychometrically calibrated verbal probability axis in frozen pooled sentence embeddings.

2. **Three-dataset psychometric triangulation.** The axis cross-validates against two independent psychometric datasets spanning over fifty years of replication. \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} → \citet{vogel2022systematic} meta-analysis: ρ = 0.991, MAE = 5.1% (mxbai-embed-large, predicative). \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} → \citet{wintle2019verbal} (n ≈ 924): ρ = 0.967, MAE = 3.6%. Convergence across datasets collected by independent groups in different decades on different populations argues against an artifact of any single calibration source.

3. **Cross-linguistic ranking transfer.** Using a multilingual embedding model (bge-m3, XLM-RoBERTa, 1024d), an axis trained on English Mosteller data ranks hedge expressions zero-shot in eight typologically diverse languages (German, French, Spanish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Arabic, Hindi), mean |ρ| = 0.928. Calibration MAE varies by language (Japanese modal MAE = 18%, Arabic modal MAE = 3.6%); we treat ranking as the trustworthy transfer claim and calibration as preliminary, pending native-speaker psychometric validation.

4. **Syntactic-type confound.** When expression types are mixed, PCA PC1 captures syntactic frame (28.7% variance), not probability; the probability signal lives on PC2. Within-type analysis is essential and uses syntax-controlled templates ("It is {X} that the experiment will succeed", "The experiment will {X} succeed", "There is a {X} that the experiment will succeed"). This finding parallels the polarity confound \citet{burger2024truth} report for truth directions in decoder LLMs.

5. **Cross-architecture robustness.** The finding holds across BERT (nomic-embed-text v1.5, 768d), BERT-large (mxbai-embed-large, 1024d), Mixture-of-Experts (nomic-v2-moe, 768d), decoder-derived embedding heads (embeddinggemma 300M, 768d; qwen3-embedding, 4096d), and multilingual XLM-R-based (bge-m3, 1024d) — five architectural families, five training procedures, one phenomenon.

6. **Functional validation via concept erasure.** Rank-1 projection of the probability axis out of sentence embeddings degrades cross-syntactic-type calibration MAE by 18–25 percentage points on the strongest-aligned axis pair (predicative ↔ modal); the magnitude of degradation correlates with inter-axis cosine alignment at Pearson $r = 0.92$ (mxbai-embed-large) / $r = 0.86$ (qwen3-embedding) across all 12 ordered cross-type pairs. The effect functionally exceeds a cosine-matched random-direction control on both models, with $z_{\text{match\_MAE}} > +2$ across all four headline pair × model × direction combinations. The probability axis is functionally carrying probability information used by downstream linear decoders, not merely correlated with embedding geometry.

7. **Null-hypothesis controls.** Permutation tests on Mosteller medians (1000 shuffles) yield p ≤ 0.005 on the predicative, frequency-adverb, and noun-phrase axes; the modal axis at n=10 falls in the small-sample rank-saturation regime (p = 0.21) and is independently validated functionally via §4.4's concept-erasure analysis. Non-epistemic adjective controls placed in the same templates produce LOO ρ = 0.31 versus 0.87 for real hedges (clean negative on the load-bearing pure-non-epistemic set; we report and address an LOO ρ = 0.69 trend on a mixed set with epistemic leakage). Random Uniform(0,100) labels match real values in ≤ 6 of 1000 trials on the three larger-n axes (p ≤ 0.006).

8. **Informative negative results.** Interpretive precision (Mosteller IQR width) is *not* in the embedding geometry (ρ ≈ 0 across four models) — embeddings encode a single stable meaning per expression rather than the spread of human judgments. Ensemble combinations across the four within-type axes do not beat the single modal axis on novel-phrase generalization. Negation breaks linear composition (cosine 0.22 vs. 0.82–0.89 for non-negated compounds), which is the geometrically appropriate behavior for a probability-reflecting axis.

## 2. Related Work

Three lines of recent work motivate, but do not subsume, the question we ask. The first establishes that pretrained language models encode many human-meaningful concepts as linear directions, but does so almost entirely on decoder LLM internal states with token-level causal intervention as the validation method. The second establishes that verbal-probability semantics are recoverable from large language models, but elicits them behaviorally through prompting rather than geometrically from representations. The third establishes that pooled sentence representations carry graded epistemic content, but for cloze probability, NLI inference strength, or rhetorical certainty rather than for the verbal-probability hedge lexicon, and not as a frozen, psychometrically calibrated linear axis. We position our contribution at the intersection these three lines leave open, and close §2 with an explicit gap statement.

### 2.1 Linear features in decoder LLM internal states

The Linear Representation Hypothesis — that human-meaningful concepts correspond to linear directions in language model representations — has been formalized and tested most extensively on decoder LLM internal states \citep{park2024linear}. \citet{marks2023geometry} identify a single "truth direction" in residual stream activations that separates true from false factual statements; \citet{burger2024truth} refine this representation to handle a polarity confound; \citet{yu2025directions} generalize one-dimensional truth directions into multi-dimensional cones for propositional facts. Within this lineage, \citet{ji2025calibrating} is the most direct framing-level comparator to the present work. They identify a single "Verbal Uncertainty Feature" (VUF) in the residual streams of Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, and Qwen-2.5-7B by computing a difference-in-means direction over question–answer pairs labeled high- versus low-verbal-uncertainty by an LLM-as-a-Judge protocol, and they validate the feature causally by steering activations along it at inference time to produce more or less hedged generations and reduce overconfident hallucinations. \citet{lepori2026fantasy} extract "modal difference vectors" from late-layer hidden states of GPT-2, Llama-3.2, OLMo-2, and Gemma-2 models that linearly discriminate four scenario-level modal categories — *probable*, *improbable*, *impossible*, *inconceivable* — across six expert-labeled datasets, demonstrate that projection magnitudes onto these vectors track fine-grained human categorization behavior, and provide preliminary causal-steering evidence that the directions are not epiphenomenal. \citet{valois2025frame} extend the LRH from single tokens to multi-token "frames" living on the unembedding matrix and demonstrate functional content via Top-*k* Concept-Guided Decoding. All four of these works share two methodological commitments: they operate on **decoder LLM internal activations** — residual streams, late-layer hidden states, unembedding matrices — and they validate functional content via **token-level causal intervention** (steering, activation patching, guided decoding), naturally available because the model generates tokens. The lines of evidence they assemble are complementary to ours within the LRH frame; the model class, the available intervention method, and (for Ji and Lepori) the calibration target differ from what we study, in ways §2.5 makes explicit.

### 2.2 Verbal-probability semantics elicited behaviorally from language models

A parallel literature investigates how language models interpret verbal probability expressions like "likely," "probably," and "highly unlikely" by **prompting** the model and comparing its numerical outputs to human survey baselines. \citet{sileo2023probing} probe pretrained models for words-of-estimative-probability classification using fine-tuned, task-specific objectives. \citet{belem2024perceptions} compare ten LLMs against 94 human participants on 14 uncertainty expressions drawn from \citet{wallsten1986measuring} and \citet{wallsten2008communicating}, framing the task as theory-of-mind ("Sonia believes it is unlikely it will rain today" — quantify Sonia's certainty, not your own) and finding that 7 of 10 LLMs map uncertainty to probabilities in human-aligned ways but are substantially more biased by prior knowledge than humans are. \citet{tang2026evaluation} evaluate 17 Words of Estimative Probability across GPT-3.5, GPT-4, two Llama-2 variants, and ERNIE-4 against a human survey baseline, finding human--LLM alignment for high-certainty expressions but divergence on the majority of mid-range expressions and additional divergence under gendered-role and Chinese-language prompting. \citet{wang2024calibrating} calibrate certainty-phrase distributions against human data using transport-based methods at the phrase level, again without embedding analysis. These works collectively establish that verbal probability semantics are quantitatively recoverable from contemporary LLMs and that human population-level probability mappings for hedge expressions are robust enough to serve as a comparison standard. They locate the phenomenon **behaviorally**, in model outputs, rather than geometrically, in model representations.

### 2.3 Probing pretrained sentence representations for human-aligned graded properties

A third, smaller line studies pooled or encoded sentence representations directly. The strongest precursor in our model class is \citet{jacobs2022masked}: mean-pooled RoBERTa sentence embeddings, with the critical final word replaced by `<mask>`, are passed through 282 leave-one-out regularized logistic-regression classifiers trained to discriminate strong- versus weak-sentential-constraint sentences from the \citet{federmeier2007multifaceted} stimulus set; the classifier's predicted probability is then correlated against the cloze probability of the masked critical word ($\hat\rho = 0.43$) and against the cloze entropy of human responses ($\hat\rho = -0.32$). The differentiation runs two layers deep. *Phenomenon:* their target is sentential constraint operationalized through next-word predictability — uncertainty about which word will come next given a preceding context — whereas our target is the verbal-probability semantics of explicit hedge expressions ("probably" → 71%, "possible" → 39%) collected from human raters. *Method:* they train per-item binary classifiers and then correlate classifier output with a continuous human signal external to the classifier; we directly regress pooled sentence embedding differences onto continuous psychometric medians, extracting a single linear axis whose calibration is the geometric object of study. They do not extract a calibrated verbal-probability axis, do not cross-validate against independent psychometric datasets, and do not test cross-linguistic transfer. \citet{chen2019uncertain} refine NLI annotation from categorical labels to continuous subjective probability via the u-SNLI dataset (61,597 MTurk-elicited probability annotations on premise–hypothesis pairs), then fine-tune scalar regression models from BERT-derived sentence representations; the phenomenon is sentence-pair entailment probability, the method is fine-tuned scalar regression rather than frozen-geometry probing, and no general probability axis is identified. \citet{schuster2019harnessing} predict human scalar pragmatic ratings (scalar implicature strength) from task-trained sentence encoders. \citet{pei2021measuring} regress continuous certainty in scientific writing from fine-tuned transformer encoders, locating rhetorical certainty rather than verbal-probability semantics. \citet{lee2015event} and \citet{stanovsky2017integrating} predict scalar factuality with structured task-specific models. \citet{bhatia2017vector} shows distributional vector-space models predict subjective probability judgments for real-world events from keyword associations, predating sentence-embedding architectures. \citet{shen2023sen2pro} layer model and data uncertainty over pretrained sentence encoders, modeling distributional embedding noise rather than human psychometric semantics. Together, these works establish that pooled sentence representations carry *some* graded epistemic content — but for cloze probability, NLI inference strength, scalar implicature, rhetorical certainty, factuality, or generic event likelihood, and not as a frozen, psychometrically calibrated verbal-probability axis cross-validated against independent psychometric datasets.

### 2.4 Verbal-probability psychometrics as ground truth

The data side of this paper draws on a sixty-year tradition of verbal-probability calibration studies. \citet{lichtenstein1967empirical} and \citet{beythmarom1982people} established that population-level mappings from verbal expressions to numerical probabilities are systematic and reproducible. \citet{wallsten1986measuring}, \citet{wallsten2008communicating}, and \citet{budescu1995processing} developed the methodological framework — non-verifiable contexts, careful statement construction, distributional rather than point estimates — that the modern literature continues to use. \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} provide the primary calibration corpus we train on: 53 verbal probability expressions rated by 238 science writers, with median, P25, P75, and IQR per expression. \citet{vogel2022systematic} is a systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 such studies spanning 1967–2018, which we use as an independent cross-validation target. \citet{wintle2019verbal} ($n \approx 924$) is a third independent dataset, collected on a different population with a different protocol, providing an additional cross-validation target. \citet{lassiter2017graded} develops the formal-semantics theory of graded modality — degree-based rather than possible-worlds — that motivates treating "probably," "possibly," and their kin as continuous-valued operators in the first place. \citet{schockaert2022formal} provides a formal framework for embeddings as epistemic states, supplying the measurement-theoretic scaffolding for the convergent-measurement perspective we adopt in §5.

### 2.5 The gap

Across these three lines, no prior work demonstrates the conjunction this paper fills:

1. **Continuously calibrated** rather than categorically classified. \citet{sileo2023probing} classify; \citet{jacobs2022masked} train binary classifiers and correlate their outputs with continuous external signals; we regress pooled embedding differences directly onto psychometric medians, on the same percentage scale humans use.
2. **Verbal probability** specifically — the hedge lexicon ("likely," "probably," "doubtful," "almost certain") — rather than truth, factuality, sentential constraint, NLI inference probability, modal categorization of event plausibility, or scalar implicature strength.
3. **Frozen pretrained pooled sentence embeddings**, rather than decoder LLM internal activations \citep{ji2025calibrating,lepori2026fantasy,marks2023geometry,yu2025directions,valois2025frame} or fine-tuned task-specific models \citep{sileo2023probing,chen2019uncertain,pei2021measuring}.
4. **Aligned with human psychometric judgments** \citep{mosteller1990quantifying,wallsten1986measuring} rather than with model-internal LLM-as-a-Judge labels \citep{ji2025calibrating} or expert-assigned modal-category labels \citep{lepori2026fantasy}.
5. **Validated against independent psychometric datasets** \citep{vogel2022systematic,wintle2019verbal}, spanning over fifty years of replication, rather than against a single calibration source.

Each qualifier on its own excludes a class of near-misses; together they identify the open territory this paper occupies. The contribution is parallel in spirit to the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature — same family of finding, namely that a graded epistemic property is recoverable as a linear direction in a pretrained representation — but is conducted in a model class where token-level causal intervention is not available, where representation-level concept erasure (§4.4) is the appropriate analogue, and where human psychometric data, rather than model behavior, is the appropriate calibration target.

## 3. Method

We extract a verbal probability axis from frozen pretrained pooled sentence embeddings, calibrate it against human psychometric medians, and evaluate it against in-sample fit, leave-one-out generalization, two independent psychometric datasets, eight typologically diverse languages, several baselines, three null-hypothesis controls, and a representation-level concept-erasure protocol. The protocol is identical across experiments: every script in the codebase is self-contained and reuses the same difference-vector construction, ridge-regression objective, and linear calibration step, parameterized only by the syntactic type of the hedge expressions and the embedding model. Math notation: vectors are column vectors, $\|\cdot\|$ is the $\ell_2$ norm, $\hat{v} = v/\|v\|$ denotes the unit-normalized form of $v$, $\rho$ is Spearman rank correlation, and MAE is mean absolute error in percentage points.

### 3.1 Data

**Primary calibration: \citet{mosteller1990quantifying}.** We use the full 53-expression \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} corpus as the primary calibration set ($n = 238$ science writers, with 25th-percentile, median, 75th-percentile, and inter-quartile-range probability values per expression on a $[0, 100]$ scale). The dataset is provided in `docs/mosteller_youtz_1990_full.csv`. We use the median as the target probability throughout; the IQR is reserved for the precision-encoding probe in §4.7.

**Cross-validation datasets.** Two independent psychometric datasets test whether the trained axis generalizes beyond the calibration source. \citet{vogel2022systematic} is a systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 verbal-probability studies spanning 1967–2018, supplied as `docs/vogel_2022_systematic_review.csv`; we extract the median pooled across studies for each expression that matches our syntactic types. \citet{wintle2019verbal} ($n \approx 924$) provides a third independent dataset; we transcribe nine predicative and three frequency-adverbial values from their Figure 2 and Table 1 (hardcoded in `experiment_08_bimodality_wintle.py`). All three sources collect aggregate human probability judgments under non-verifiable contexts following the methodological framework of \citet{wallsten1986measuring} and \citet{budescu1995processing}; the convergence of three datasets collected by different groups in different decades on different populations is the basis for the cross-dataset generalization claim in §4.2.

**Type classification.** Mixing syntactic types confounds the analysis: PCA on the 52 mixed-type Mosteller difference vectors recovers PC1 as a syntactic-frame direction (28.7% of variance, ordering noun-phrase constructions against frequency adverbs) rather than as the probability axis (FINDINGS-01). Within-type analysis is therefore not a methodological convenience but a load-bearing decision that isolates the semantic signal from the syntactic envelope (§4.1; cf. the polarity confound \citealp{burger2024truth} report for truth directions). We partition the Mosteller expressions into four syntactic types, each with a single sentence template that holds syntax constant across all expressions of that type. Let $T_{\text{type}}(x)$ denote the template for syntactic type with hedge expression $x$ substituted for the placeholder, and let $s_0$ denote the bare claim "The experiment will succeed." The four types and their templates are:

- **Predicative adjective** ($n = 13$): $T_{\text{pred}}(x) = $ "It is $x$ that the experiment will succeed."
- **Frequency adverb** ($n = 19$): $T_{\text{freq}}(x) = $ "The experiment will $x$ succeed."
- **Noun phrase** ($n = 11$): $T_{\text{np}}(x) = $ "There is a(n) $x$ that the experiment will succeed."
- **Modal adverb** ($n = 10$): $T_{\text{mod}}(x) = $ "The experiment will $x$ succeed."

The frequency-adverb and modal-adverb templates share a syntactic slot but encode different semantic types (base-rate frequency versus epistemic confidence); the modal axis is not derivable from the frequency axis (§4.1, FINDINGS-03). Modal training is restricted to the ten expressions with Mosteller-grounded probabilities — adverbial forms of Mosteller predicative adjectives such as `certainly`, `probably`, and `possibly` — to keep the calibration target purely psychometric. The seven additional modal adverbs used in early exploratory work (`perhaps`, `conceivably`, `arguably`, etc.) carry author-estimated probabilities and are excluded from the load-bearing axis training; they reappear as held-out novel phrases in §4.5.

### 3.2 Models

We test six pretrained embedding models, accessed through the Ollama embedding endpoint at `http://localhost:11434/api/embed`. Five models are used for the within-type analyses; the sixth (bge-m3) supports the multilingual experiment. We make no fine-tuning, no head training, and no architectural modifications; the qualifier "frozen pretrained" in the novelty claim depends on this.

| Model | Dimension | Architecture | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| nomic-embed-text v1.5 | 768 | BERT + Matryoshka | Default |
| embeddinggemma 300M | 768 | Decoder-derived (Gemma) | Architectural variety |
| nomic-embed-text v2 MoE | 768 | Mixture of Experts | Architectural variety |
| mxbai-embed-large | 1024 | BERT-large | Architectural variety |
| qwen3-embedding | 4096 | Qwen3 | Larger capacity |
| bge-m3 | 1024 | XLM-RoBERTa, multilingual | Cross-linguistic transfer (§4.3) |

The five within-type models span five distinct architectural families (BERT, BERT-large, Mixture of Experts, decoder-derived heads, and Qwen3) and three training paradigms; bge-m3 adds a multilingual XLM-R-based encoder trained on more than one hundred languages. All five within-type models output a single mean- or [CLS]-pooled sentence vector per input. Cross-architecture robustness (§4.1) is the basis for the claim that the verbal probability axis is a property of pretrained pooled-embedding geometry rather than of any specific architecture or training corpus.

### 3.3 Axis extraction

**Difference-vector construction.** For each syntactic type $T$ with expressions $\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ and Mosteller medians $\{y_1, \ldots, y_n\}$, we embed the bare claim $s_0$ and each templated sentence $T(x_i)$ using the model's pooled output, yielding $\mathbf{e}_0$ and $\{\mathbf{e}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{e}_n\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$. The input feature for expression $x_i$ is the difference vector
$$
\mathbf{d}_i \;=\; \mathbf{e}_i - \mathbf{e}_0,
$$
which subtracts off the propositional content shared across all expressions in the type and isolates the contribution of the hedge phrase. Differencing against a shared bare claim is the same construction \citet{mikolov2013linguistic} used to expose word-level analogies and that the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature uses to extract truth and uncertainty directions from residual streams \citep{marks2023geometry,ji2025calibrating}.

**Ridge regression on Mosteller medians.** We standardize the medians $\tilde{y}_i = (y_i - \bar{y})/\mathrm{std}(y)$ and stack the difference vectors as rows of $D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$. The probability axis is the unit-normalized solution to a ridge regression \citep{hoerl1970ridge} with regularization $\lambda = 0.1$:
$$
\mathbf{w}^{*} \;=\; \bigl(D^{\top} D + \lambda I_d\bigr)^{-1} D^{\top} \tilde{\mathbf{y}}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{w}} \;=\; \mathbf{w}^{*} / \|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|.
$$
Solving the linear system in $d$ dimensions on $n \le 19$ samples is severely under-determined; the ridge term selects the minimum-norm solution along the dominant probability-correlated direction in the column span of $D$ and stabilizes against numerical conditioning issues at the high embedding dimensions ($d \in \{768, 1024, 4096\}$). The choice $\lambda = 0.1$ is fixed across all experiments and all models for cross-cell comparability rather than per-cell optimum: a four-decade sweep on $\lambda \in \{0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10\}$ across nomic-embed-text v1.5 and mxbai-embed-large by all four within-type axes (eight cells) confirms that LOO Spearman $\rho$ remains within 0.072 of the $\lambda = 0.1$ reference everywhere, with the worst-case drop on nomic predicative ($\rho = 0.874$ at $\lambda = 0.1$ falling to $0.802$ at $\lambda = 10$). The sweep is asymmetric, however: on six of the eight (model $\times$ type) cells a smaller $\lambda$ would yield a higher LOO $\rho$ than $\lambda = 0.1$, and the modal axis is the cell where regularizer choice does the most rank shuffling — most strikingly mxbai modal, where the reference $\lambda = 0.1$ sits at the sweep minimum (LOO $\rho = 0.806$, vs. $0.903$ at $\lambda = 0.001$ and $0.869$ at $\lambda = 10$), consistent with the small-$n$ rank-saturation regime developed in §6 (L7).

**Linear calibration.** The unit axis $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ ranks expressions correctly but does not return probabilities on the percentage scale humans use. We recover the calibration via ordinary least squares on the in-sample projections $p_i = \mathbf{d}_i^{\top} \hat{\mathbf{w}}$ against the unstandardized Mosteller medians $y_i$:
$$
\hat{y}_i \;=\; \alpha \cdot \mathbf{d}_i^{\top} \hat{\mathbf{w}} + \beta, \qquad \text{with } (\alpha, \beta) \text{ from } \mathrm{OLS}\bigl(\{p_i\}, \{y_i\}\bigr).
$$
Predicted probabilities are clipped to $[0, 100]$. The calibration is a one-dimensional affine map; it cannot improve ranking over the unit projection but is what allows continuous predictions on the same percentage scale used in psychometric reporting, and is what the abstract's "continuously calibrated" qualifier refers to. We train one $(\hat{\mathbf{w}}, \alpha, \beta)$ triple per (model, syntactic type) pair, yielding twenty axes for the within-type analyses (five models × four types) plus the eight cross-linguistic axes (bge-m3 × eight languages × {predicative, modal}). Sentence templates use a single propositional content ("the experiment will succeed") for the load-bearing analyses; FINDINGS-01 reports cross-content validation showing axis ranking $\rho > 0.69$ on alternative bare claims ("the treatment will be effective", "the prediction will be correct").

### 3.4 Evaluation

Five evaluation strands address generalization, baselines, null hypotheses, cross-linguistic transfer, and functional content respectively. All Spearman correlations and MAE values are computed against held-out targets except the in-sample diagnostics explicitly labeled as such.

**In-sample fit and leave-one-out generalization.** For each (model, type) we report in-sample Spearman $\rho$ between calibrated predictions and Mosteller medians, and leave-one-out (LOO) Spearman $\rho$ obtained by retraining $(\hat{\mathbf{w}}, \alpha, \beta)$ on the $n-1$ remaining expressions, predicting the held-out one, and correlating the $n$ LOO predictions against the medians. We complement LOO $\rho$ with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals over 10,000 resamples of the (prediction, median) pairs (`paper_validation.py:spearman_ci_bootstrap`, fixed seed 42). For psychometric ground truths with $n = 10$–$19$ per type, the LOO–bootstrap pairing is the appropriate generalization summary; the small $n$ regime saturates rank-based metrics when in-sample $\rho \geq 0.95$, a methodological constraint we return to in the concept-erasure analysis (§4.4) and limitations (§6).

**Cross-dataset evaluation.** The Mosteller-trained axis is applied without retraining to two independent psychometric datasets. For \citet{vogel2022systematic}, expressions are matched by surface form and embedded in the same syntactic templates as the Mosteller training data; predicted probabilities are compared against Vogel medians via Spearman $\rho$ and MAE. The \citet{wintle2019verbal} protocol is identical, applied separately to predicative and frequency-adverbial expressions. No expression overlap between training and test is filtered: we treat the two datasets as offering the same population-level mapping refined on different samples, and Mosteller→Vogel and Mosteller→Wintle correlations as the appropriate measure of population-level recovery rather than as out-of-sample novelty tests. Novel-phrase generalization beyond the psychometric corpora is handled separately in §4.5 using author-rated targets.

**Baselines.** For each syntactic type we report three unsupervised baselines alongside the supervised ridge axis, all using the same calibration step (3.3) for fair comparison:

1. **Random direction.** A unit vector drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ and normalized; we report mean and standard deviation across 100 independent samples (`paper_validation.py:run_baselines`, fixed seed 42).
2. **Mean-difference direction.** $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{mean}} = \widehat{\overline{\mathbf{d}}}$ where $\overline{\mathbf{d}}$ is the centroid of the difference vectors. This is the unsupervised counterpart of the ridge axis and the construction used by \citet{marks2023geometry} and \citet{ji2025calibrating} for difference-in-means truth and uncertainty features.
3. **PCA PC1.** The first principal component of the centered difference matrix, capturing the dominant direction of variation regardless of probability ground truth.

The baseline comparison addresses whether the supervised signal is genuinely probability-aligned: a random direction with the same calibration step bounds the floor; PC1 indicates how much of the probability signal is recoverable without supervision; the mean-difference direction tests whether a single "hedge insertion" centroid suffices in the absence of probability labels.

**Null hypothesis controls.** Three controls (`experiment_10_null_hypothesis.py`) test whether the observed effect could arise from the protocol independently of the hedge semantics: (i) a permutation test that shuffles Mosteller medians among the expressions and re-runs LOO 1{,}000 times, reporting the empirical $p$-value of the observed real-label $\rho$; (ii) a non-epistemic adjective control that substitutes adjectives without epistemic content (e.g., scenic, metallic) into the same predicative template and trains a putative "axis" against author-assigned arbitrary ratings; (iii) a random-label control that draws Uniform$(0, 100)$ values per expression, runs the full ridge-plus-LOO pipeline, and tabulates how often the random-label LOO $\rho$ exceeds the real-label LOO $\rho$ across 1{,}000 trials. Results in §4.6.

**Cross-linguistic protocol.** Using bge-m3 (1024d, XLM-R, multilingual; \citealp{chen2024bge}), we train predicative and modal axes on English Mosteller data following §3.3, then apply each axis without retraining to translated hedge expressions in eight typologically diverse languages: German, French, Spanish, Mandarin Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Arabic, and Hindi. Each language uses a translated bare claim and translated syntactic templates analogous to the English ones; difference vectors are computed against the language-matched bare claim before projection onto the English-trained axis. The primary metric is Spearman $\rho$ between ranked projections and the English Mosteller equivalents of each translated expression — a *ranking* transfer claim that does not depend on the translation preserving exact numerical calibration. We report MAE as a secondary diagnostic. The conditions under which native-speaker calibration would be required for a valid MAE comparison, and the resulting limits on what transfers and what does not, are discussed in §6 (L2). Implementation: `experiment_09_cross_linguistic.py`.

**Concept erasure.** As the embedding-class analogue of the causal-intervention protocols standard in the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature \citep{ji2025calibrating,marks2023geometry,valois2025frame}, we test whether the probability axis is functional rather than merely correlated by erasing it from one syntactic type's embeddings via rank-1 projection and measuring degradation of probability prediction along a different type's axis. For ordered pair $(A, B)$ with $A, B \in \{\text{predicative}, \text{frequency}, \text{noun phrase}, \text{modal}\}$ and $A \neq B$, we erase the type-$A$ axis $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A$ from each type-$B$ difference vector via the rank-1 projection
$$
\mathbf{d}_i' \;=\; \mathbf{d}_i - (\mathbf{d}_i^{\top} \hat{\mathbf{w}}_A)\, \hat{\mathbf{w}}_A,
$$
then re-decode with type $B$'s calibrated triple $(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_B, \alpha_B, \beta_B)$ and compare in-sample $\rho$ and MAE before and after erasure. Geometric overlap alone — erasing any direction with non-trivial $\cos(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A, \hat{\mathbf{w}}_B)$ — would degrade prediction; the relevant question is whether erasing the *probability* axis degrades prediction *more* than erasing a direction with the same overlap. We therefore compare against a cosine-matched random control: 100 unit directions $\mathbf{r}$ constructed so that $|\cos(\mathbf{r}, \hat{\mathbf{w}}_B)| = |\cos(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A, \hat{\mathbf{w}}_B)|$ by sampling $\mathbf{r} = \pm c\,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_B + \sqrt{1 - c^2}\,\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\perp}$ with $c = |\cos(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A, \hat{\mathbf{w}}_B)|$ and $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\perp}$ a random unit vector orthogonal to $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_B$. The two-part design — real-axis rank-1 erasure plus cosine-matched random null — is what isolates *functional* probability content from geometric overlap. Full results, the choice of MAE versus $\rho$ as the load-bearing metric under small-$n$ rank-saturation, and the cross-pair $\Delta\mathrm{MAE}$ versus inter-axis cosine signature are presented in §4.4. Implementation: `experiment_11_concept_erasure.py`, run on mxbai-embed-large (1024d) and qwen3-embedding (4096d) for cross-architecture robustness.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 The syntactic confound and within-type probability axes

When verbal probability expressions are pooled across the four syntactic types of §3.1, the dominant linear direction in the embedding space is *not* probability but syntax. PCA on the 52 mixed-type difference vectors $\{\mathbf{d}_i\}$ from nomic-embed-text v1.5 returns a first principal component that explains 28.7% of the variance, and inspection of its extremes orders constructions by template family rather than by Mosteller median: noun-phrase formulations such as "high probability," "very high probability," and "moderate probability" sit at one end, frequency adverbs such as "very infrequent," "not often," and "not very often" at the other. The probability signal appears only on PC2 (14.8% of variance, $\rho = -0.68$ against the medians, $p = 2.4 \times 10^{-8}$), and PC1 itself correlates only weakly with the medians ($\rho = -0.28$, $p = 0.05$). The *largest* axis of variation in the cross-type difference-vector cloud is therefore a syntactic-frame direction onto which probability projects only as a secondary signal. This is the structural counterpart in pretrained pooled sentence embeddings of the polarity confound \citet{burger2024truth} report for truth directions in decoder LLM residual streams: a candidate semantic direction recovered without controlling for a co-varying syntactic property is contaminated by that property.

Holding the syntactic type fixed reverses this picture. Within each of the four type partitions, a single ridge-regression axis $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ trained on Mosteller medians by the protocol of §3.3 recovers the probability ordering with high in-sample fidelity and generalizes through leave-one-out (LOO) cross-validation. Table 1 reports both metrics across the five within-type embedding models. In-sample Spearman $\rho$ exceeds 0.90 for every (model, type) cell — the supervised claim foregrounded in the abstract — and LOO $\rho$ falls in the range 0.59–0.94, with embeddinggemma's predicative axis reaching the highest in-sample fit (0.99) and nomic-embed-text v1.5's noun-phrase axis reaching the highest LOO ($\rho = 0.94$). The two LOO cells below 0.65 (embeddinggemma modal at 0.59 and nomic-embed-text v1.5 modal at 0.65) are the small-sample saturation regime $\rho$-based metrics enter at $n = 10$ training items; we discuss this and its implications for the §4.4 concept-erasure analysis in §6 (L7). The dominance of probability on the within-type difference-vector cloud is also visible without supervision: PCA PC1 within type reaches $\rho = 0.98$ against the Mosteller medians on noun phrases for nomic-embed-text v1.5 (PC1 explains 36% of the within-type variance), $\rho = 0.93$ for predicative on mxbai-embed-large (PC1 = 60%), and $\rho = 0.96$ for noun phrase on qwen3-embedding (PC1 = 61%). ### 4.2 Three-dataset psychometric triangulation

The \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} calibration is sixty years old and has been replicated repeatedly under different protocols and on different populations. We test whether the within-type axes trained on Mosteller medians transfer, *without retraining*, to two independently collected psychometric corpora: the \citet{vogel2022systematic} systematic review and meta-analysis aggregating 21 verbal-probability studies from 1967 to 2018, and the \citet{wintle2019verbal} experiment ($n \approx 924$) drawing on a different population with a different elicitation protocol. The Mosteller-trained axis $(\hat{\mathbf{w}}, \alpha, \beta)$ is applied as in §3.4 to surface-form-matched expressions embedded in the same syntactic templates; we report Spearman $\rho$ and MAE on the Vogel and Wintle medians.

For predicative expressions — the type with the largest cross-corpus overlap ($n_{\text{test}} = 11$ for Vogel, 9 for Wintle) — Mosteller-to-Vogel correlations exceed 0.89 across all five models and reach 0.991 on mxbai-embed-large with MAE = 5.1 percentage points (Table 2). Noun-phrase expressions have no cross-corpus matches and are not included in the table; the cross-corpus tests therefore probe predicative and frequency-adverbial calibration directly.

### 4.4 Functional validation via concept erasure

The §3.4 protocol — rank-1 erasure of axis $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A$ from type-$B$ difference vectors followed by re-decoding with type $B$'s calibrated triple — applied across all twelve ordered pairs $(A, B)$ with $A \neq B$ and $A, B \in \{\text{predicative}, \text{frequency adverb}, \text{noun phrase}, \text{modal}\}$ on mxbai-embed-large (1024d) and qwen3-embedding (4096d), yields a single cross-model structural signature. The Pearson correlation between inter-axis cosine alignment $\cos(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A, \hat{\mathbf{w}}_B)$ and the increase in calibrated mean absolute prediction error after erasure ($\Delta\mathrm{MAE}$) is $r = 0.92$ on mxbai-embed-large and $r = 0.86$ on qwen3-embedding (Figure 13). Erasing a probability-aligned axis from cross-type embeddings degrades the downstream linear decoder in proportion to the probability content the eraser shares with the target, and this scaling holds across two architecturally distinct models — a 1024-dimensional BERT-large variant and a 4096-dimensional decoder-derived embedding head. We treat cross-model agreement on this cross-pair signature, rather than per-pair functional verdicts, as the load-bearing finding: the four within-type axes carry probability information that downstream decoders use, and the use is graded by geometric alignment in the way one would predict if the four axes were partially redundant projections of a shared epistemic dimension (§5).

The predicative–modal pair, calibrated to the same Mosteller medians in adjective and adverb form respectively, has the strongest a priori reason to share content; its cosine alignment is the highest of the twelve ordered pairs in both models ($\cos = 0.88$ on mxbai-embed-large, $0.79$ on qwen3-embedding). Erasing the predicative axis from modal-type embeddings raises MAE from 6.8 to 25.2 percentage points on mxbai ($\Delta\mathrm{MAE} = +18.4$) and from 5.2 to 22.5 on qwen3 ($+17.4$); the reverse direction (erase modal, decode with predicative) raises MAE from 4.6 to 29.2 on mxbai ($+24.6$) and from 5.5 to 26.0 on qwen3 ($+20.5$). All four headline pair $\times$ model $\times$ direction combinations exceed the cosine-matched random-direction null, with real-axis $\Delta\mathrm{MAE}$ producing 2--23\% more MAE damage than matched-random direction erasure (ratios of $1.02$, $1.12$, $1.17$, and $1.23$ across the four combinations); the corresponding $z_{\text{match\_MAE}}$ values clear $+2$ in every case but are inflated where the matched-null standard deviation is small at high cosine, so the ratios are the more interpretable summary. The matched-random control is by construction stringent at high cosine — at $\cos = 0.88$ the matched direction reproduces approximately 98\% of the real-axis MAE damage by geometric overlap alone (§6 L6) — so the headline pair clearing this control on both models in both directions is meaningful evidence of probability content beyond geometric correlation.

We report MAE rather than $\rho$ as the load-bearing metric throughout §4.4 because in-sample $\rho$ already reaches $\geq 0.95$ across all four within-type axes in both models, leaving rank-based metrics with no headroom to register erasure effects: rank-1 projection compresses magnitudes while preserving order, and Spearman $\rho$ is insensitive to that compression (§6 L7). The $\rho$-only view of the same twelve ordered pairs returns five functional pairs on mxbai and three on qwen3 with zero overlap — apparent cross-model disagreement that resolves into agreement under MAE: nine of twelve ordered pairs are MAE-functional in both models simultaneously, and the cross-pair correlations $r(\cos, \Delta\mathrm{MAE}) = +0.92$ and $+0.86$ contrast with $r(\cos, \Delta\rho) = -0.72$ and $+0.24$. Per-pair functional verdicts on individual low-cosine pairs are below the experiment's resolving power, particularly where the matched-random control is most over-conservative (§6 L6); the cross-pair pattern is the structural observation. The §4.4 result is the embedding-class structural analogue of the activation-steering and representation-patching protocols standard in the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature \citep{ji2025calibrating,marks2023geometry,valois2025frame,lepori2026fantasy}: rank-1 representation-level erasure under a calibration-aware downstream linear decoder plays the role causal intervention plays in token-generating models, with the methodological framing developed in §5 and the necessary "analogue rather than substitute" caveat in §6 L5.

### 4.5 Robustness: dimensional truncation, novel-phrase generalization, compound composition, bimodality probe

Four robustness probes test the verbal probability axis along axes orthogonal to the Mosteller-Vogel-Wintle calibration: dimensional truncation, novel-phrase generalization, compound-hedge composition, and the bimodal-modifier "possible" probe. Each is consistent with the headline within-type and cross-dataset results of §4.1–§4.2 and constrains the interpretation of those results in a different direction.

**Matryoshka dimensional truncation.** Following the Matryoshka Representation Learning protocol \citep{kusupati2022matryoshka}, we re-extract the within-type axes after truncating each model's pooled embedding to 512, 256, 128, 64, and 32 dimensions and recomputing the difference vectors and ridge axis at each truncated dimensionality. On nomic-embed-text v1.5 (768d, MRL-trained), worst-case LOO $\rho$ across the four within-type axes drops by only 0.027 at 64 dimensions — a 12$\times$ compression that preserves rank-based recovery of the probability axis on every type. Even at 32 dimensions (24$\times$ compression), the worst-case LOO $\rho$ drop remains 0.116, with the modal axis actually *improving* slightly (0.948 $\to$ 0.960). On mxbai-embed-large (1024d, not MRL-trained), the axis is robust to 128 dimensions (8$\times$) but degrades at 64 dimensions (worst-case LOO $\rho$ drop = 0.154), as expected for a model whose loss objective does not enforce nested-prefix sufficiency. Axis-stability cosines between the full-dimensional axis and its truncated counterpart confirm this dichotomy: nomic at 64 dimensions retains cos $\geq 0.94$ to its full-dimensional axis on every type, mxbai cos $\geq 0.92$ at 128 but falls to 0.85–0.93 at 32. Figure 5 plots the full LOO $\rho$ versus dimensionality curve. The MRL-versus-non-MRL contrast is the methodological observation: where a model has been explicitly trained for nested-prefix generality, the verbal probability axis inherits that property without retraining.

![Matryoshka dimensional truncation: LOO $\rho$ versus truncated embedding dimension on nomic-embed-text v1.5 (MRL-trained, 768d) and mxbai-embed-large (non-MRL, 1024d). The MRL-trained model preserves the axis to 64 dimensions (12$\times$ compression); the non-MRL model holds at 128 dimensions.](figures/fig5_truncation.pdf){#fig:truncation}

**Novel-phrase generalization.** The Mosteller calibration covers 53 expressions, none of them informal first-person hedges ("I think," "I believe"), doubt expressions ("I doubt," "I wonder if"), anti-hedges ("obviously," "clearly," "without question"), or constructions outside the survey's vocabulary ("signs point to," "I'd wager," "it stands to reason"). To test whether the trained axes encode probability semantics or only Mosteller-vocabulary lookup, we apply each model's trained axes to a held-out set of 26 such phrases embedded as natural sentences and correlate against author-rated intuition probabilities. Spearman $\rho$ across the 26 phrases reaches 0.752 on nomic-embed-text v1.5, 0.842 on mxbai-embed-large, 0.831 on nomic v2 MoE, 0.870 on embeddinggemma, and 0.895 on qwen3-embedding; on the ten purely novel phrases (the "novel" subset, excluding the informal-hedge and anti-hedge categories), the same correlations rise to 0.796–0.942. The geometry orders informal first-person hedges intuitively (believe > think > imagine > suspect > wonder > doubt > not sure) and correctly assigns sub-50% probabilities to doubt expressions and high-50s/60s to anti-hedges, with a systematic compression effect — natural sentence constructions distribute the difference vector across more dimensions than the controlled templates do, attenuating projection magnitudes toward the center while preserving rank order. The author-rated ground truth limits the strength of the calibration claim; the result is informative as evidence that the axis ranks out-of-vocabulary phrases sensibly, not as a calibrated psychometric mapping for those phrases. We make this distinction explicit and treat the §4.5 numbers as evidence of generalization rather than as calibrated-prediction claims (§6 L3).

**Compound-hedge composition.** Natural language stacks hedge cues — "I think it will probably succeed," "It seems fairly likely" — and the question is whether the difference vector for a compound hedge equals the sum of its component vectors. On qwen3-embedding and mxbai-embed-large, the cosine between a compound difference vector $\mathbf{d}_{A+B}$ and its component sum $\mathbf{d}_A + \mathbf{d}_B$ falls in the range 0.82–0.89 across most compound types (first-person + modal, evidential + modal, attenuator + predicative, double first-person), with a systematic magnitude ratio of approximately 0.75 — the compound is sub-linear in magnitude but near-linear in direction. Negation is the structural exception: $\mathbf{d}_{\neg A}$ is near-orthogonal to $\mathbf{d}_A$ (cos = 0.22 on qwen3-embedding for "not impossible" versus "impossible") rather than antiparallel, and a follow-up probe (FINDINGS-06) finds that negation acts as a *probability-reflecting* operation — negating a high-probability word produces a vector aligned with the negation of other high-probability words ($+0.96$ between "not likely" and "not probable") but anti-correlated with negations of low-probability words ($-0.93$ between "not possible" and "not impossible"). Negation is not a single direction in the embedding, but its action on the verbal probability axis is structured: it inverts the projection. This is the geometrically appropriate behavior for a probability-reflecting axis and constrains how a downstream calibrator should handle negated hedges (Figure 11).

![Compound-hedge composition: cosine between $\mathbf{d}_{A+B}$ and $\mathbf{d}_A + \mathbf{d}_B$ across compound types on qwen3-embedding and mxbai-embed-large. Most compounds compose near-linearly (cos $\geq$ 0.82); negation (cos = 0.22 on qwen3) is the principled exception.](figures/fig11_compound.pdf){#fig:compound}

**Bimodal-modifier "possible" probe.** The Mosteller distribution for "possible" is bimodal — one human cluster reads it as logical possibility (5–10%), another as reasonable chance (40–50%) — and the axis, as the IQR result of §4.7 will show, encodes a single point estimate per expression. We probe whether modifiers can disambiguate this in geometry by embedding "{modifier} possible" with twelve modifiers spanning low-leaning ("barely," "just barely," "remotely") to high-leaning ("entirely," "very," "eminently"). On mxbai-embed-large, low-leaning compositions of "possible" project to 0.1–13.4% on the predicative axis and high-leaning compositions to 60.3–74.1%, a spread of 31.6 percentage points (Spearman $\rho = 0.893$ between projection and expected probability across the twelve modifiers). On qwen3-embedding the spread is 29.7 percentage points; on nomic-embed-text v1.5 it is 20.0. The geometry traverses the bimodality range when a disambiguating modifier is present even though it cannot represent the bare-"possible" ambiguity directly. Figure 10 visualizes the spread.

Low-leaning compositions ("barely possible") project to 0.1–13.4%; high-leaning compositions ("eminently possible") to 60.3–74.1%. Spread 20–32pp.](figures/fig10_bimodality.pdf){#fig:bimodality}

### 4.6 Null-hypothesis controls

Three controls (`experiment_10_null_hypothesis.py`, on nomic-embed-text v1.5) establish that the within-type LOO recovery of §4.1 is in the phrase-to-probability mapping rather than in the protocol or template structure.

**Permutation test.** Randomly permuting the Mosteller medians among the expressions of each type and rerunning the full ridge-plus-LOO pipeline 1,000 times yields a permuted-LOO-$\rho$ distribution centered near zero with standard deviation 0.29–0.42 per type. For predicative, frequency-adverb, and noun-phrase axes the real-label LOO $\rho$ exceeds the 99th percentile of the permuted distribution: predicative real $\rho = 0.874$ at the 99.7th percentile ($p = 0.003$), frequency adverb real $\rho = 0.923$ at the 100th percentile ($p < 0.001$), noun phrase real $\rho = 0.936$ at the 99.5th percentile ($p = 0.005$). The modal axis at $n = 10$ does not reach significance under permutation (real $\rho = 0.649$ at the 79.2nd percentile, $p = 0.21$): with only ten Mosteller-grounded modal expressions, the permuted distribution has high variance, and a non-trivial fraction of label permutations produces LOO $\rho$ above 0.6 by chance. This is an instance of the small-$n$ rank-saturation regime §6 L7 develops in full. The modal axis is independently validated as functional in §4.4 via the concept-erasure analysis on $n = 10$, where the cosine-matched random-direction null distinguishes it cleanly from a geometric correlate; we read the §4.6 permutation result as a low-power test at small $n$ rather than as evidence that the modal axis lacks signal. For the three larger-$n$ axes, the phrase-to-probability mapping is not a property of the templates.

**Non-epistemic adjective control.** A *pure* set of thirteen non-epistemic adjectives ("expensive," "complicated," "old," "fast," "difficult," and similar items with no epistemic content), placed in the same predicative template and trained against author-assigned arbitrary labels, returns LOO $\rho = 0.306$ — well within the random-label null distribution and cleanly below the real-epistemic predicative LOO $\rho = 0.874$. The script's *mixed* set, by contrast, returns LOO $\rho = 0.69$ and triggers a "WARNING: Non-epistemic adjectives show some structure" line in the raw output. The mixed set was constructed at the boundary of epistemic content and includes adjectives like "expected," "obvious," "documented," and "unprecedented," several of which are compositionally near-synonymous with Mosteller items. We retain that warning rather than suppressing it and treat the mixed-set $\rho = 0.69$ as evidence *for* the geometry's epistemic sensitivity — the axis picks up residual epistemic loading in items like "expected" ($\approx$ "probable") and "obvious" ($\approx$ "certain"). The pure set is the load-bearing comparison; §6 L4 develops this pre-emption in full.

**Random-label control.** Drawing labels from Uniform$(0, 100)$ for the real Mosteller expressions and rerunning the ridge-plus-LOO pipeline 1,000 times per type yields a random-label LOO $\rho$ distribution that matches the real-label LOO $\rho$ in 6 of 1,000 trials for predicative ($p = 0.006$), 0 of 1,000 for frequency adverb ($p < 0.001$), and 3 of 1,000 for noun phrase ($p = 0.003$). The modal axis at $n = 10$ matches in 197 of 1,000 trials ($p = 0.20$), the same low-power signature as the permutation test above. The signal in the three larger-$n$ axes is in the phrase-probability mapping the model learned during pretraining, not in the structural properties of the regression.

Real LOO $\rho$ exceeds the 99th percentile of the permuted distribution on the three larger-$n$ axes (predicative, frequency adverb, noun phrase; $p \leq 0.005$); the modal axis at $n = 10$ shows the small-sample rank-saturation regime §6 L7 develops, where high-variance permuted distributions overlap moderately with the real-label point estimate.](figures/fig7_null_hypothesis.pdf){#fig:null-hypothesis}

### 4.7 Negative results

Two informative negatives constrain the interpretation of the verbal probability axis: the IQR result tells us what kind of object the axis is, and the ensemble result tells us that the four within-type axes do not stack into a more accurate combined predictor.

**Interpretive precision is not in the geometry.** \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} report the inter-quartile range (IQR) of human probability ratings per expression alongside the median; "possible" has IQR = 42.7 (P25 = 7.5%, P75 = 50.2%, the bimodal distribution probed in §4.5), while "certain" has IQR = 1.1. We test whether the embedding geometry encodes this interpretive precision by computing the cross-claim consistency of each expression's difference vector — its mean pairwise cosine across 15 propositional contents — and correlating against $-$IQR. If the geometry encoded which expressions humans agree about, low-IQR expressions should produce more consistent cross-claim directions. Across four models (mxbai-embed-large, qwen3-embedding, nomic v2 MoE, embeddinggemma) the correlation $\rho(\text{consistency}, -\text{IQR})$ ranges from $-0.20$ to $+0.05$ with $p > 0.5$ in every case. The geometry encodes a single stable meaning per expression, not the spread of human interpretations of that expression. This is informative for what the verbal probability axis *is*: a point estimator on the population probability mapping, not a representation of the variance of that mapping. A practical calibration system that needs interpretive-precision flags must read them from external psychometric data (the Mosteller IQR column) rather than from the axis. The result is also a constraint on the formal-semantics interpretation in §5: the axis is consistent with treating "probably," "possibly," and their kin as continuous-valued operators on a single probability dimension \citep{lassiter2017graded}, not with treating them as distributional objects encoding rater variance.

**No ensemble of within-type axes beats the single modal axis on novel phrases.** §4.1 yields four within-type axes per model (predicative, frequency adverb, noun phrase, modal). One natural follow-up — given that these axes are moderately correlated (cos = 0.41–0.88 within a single model on mxbai; cf. §5) — is to combine them into a single ensemble predictor. We tested ridge-regressed linear ensemble, magnitude-weighted average, max-projection routing, calibrated logistic stacking, and a naïve four-axis mean against the modal axis alone on the novel-phrase set of §4.5. No ensemble strategy beats the modal axis alone: on mxbai-embed-large the modal-only MAE is 9.7%, the best ensemble (linear ridge) reaches 11.4%, and the oracle that knows the ground truth and picks the best axis per phrase reaches 5.9%. The pattern repeats on qwen3-embedding (modal 5.9%, best ensemble 8.3%, oracle 4.3%) and embeddinggemma (modal 5.6%, best ensemble 7.5%, oracle 4.5%). The modal axis is within 1–4 percentage points of oracle on every model and is the practical fallback for arbitrary natural hedge phrasings. The ensemble's failure to add value is interpretable: the four axes are not independent measurements of a shared latent quantity but partially redundant projections of one underlying epistemic dimension whose modal-frame instantiation happens to dominate the syntactic distribution of natural hedging.

## 5. Discussion

The recent linear-feature interpretability literature — \citet{marks2023geometry}, \citet{burger2024truth}, \citet{yu2025directions}, \citet{ji2025calibrating}, \citet{lepori2026fantasy}, and \citet{valois2025frame} — has documented calibrated linear structure for graded epistemic properties almost exclusively on decoder LLM internal states: residual streams, late-layer hidden states, and unembedding matrices. In each of these settings the natural protocol for separating a functional concept direction from a merely correlated direction is token-level causal intervention — activation steering, residual-stream patching, guided decoding — because the model generates tokens and the consequence of perturbing an internal state can be read off the output distribution. Pretrained pooled sentence embedding models are categorically different objects: they compress an input sentence to a single vector for retrieval or similarity, expose no token-generation pathway, and do not admit the steering-style interventions that ground the decoder-class results. The contribution of this paper is to demonstrate that analogous calibrated linear structure exists in this model class, and that doing so honestly requires two adaptations of the decoder-LLM playbook. The validation method is the first: rank-1 representation-level erasure of $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A$ from a target type's difference vectors, evaluated against a calibration-aware downstream linear decoder and a cosine-matched random-direction control (§4.4), is the embedding-class structural analogue of activation steering. The calibration target is the second: rather than the model-internal LLM-as-a-Judge verbal-uncertainty scores \citet{ji2025calibrating} calibrate to, our axis is calibrated to human psychometric medians from \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} and cross-validated against \citet{vogel2022systematic} and \citet{wintle2019verbal}, datasets that span sixty years of replication on independent populations. We frame the §4.4 result as the model-class analogue of decoder-LLM causal intervention rather than as a substitute for it (§6 L5); what it establishes, in conjunction with the calibration result, is that the empirical phenomenon documented in the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature has a counterpart in pretrained pooled sentence embeddings, validated by the appropriate analogues at every step.

The four within-type axes recovered in §4.1 are not independent measurements but constituent directions in a low-dimensional epistemic subspace. Pairwise cosine alignment between the axes is moderate (cf. §4.7's mxbai-embed-large range of 0.41–0.88), and the predicative–modal pair — calibrated to the same Mosteller medians in adjective and adverb form respectively — is the most aligned in every model tested, with that alignment increasing systematically with model capacity (smaller models near 0.5, mxbai-embed-large at 0.88, qwen3-embedding at 0.79; §4.4 reports the latter two from the post-erasure ridge fit). This pattern parallels \citet{yu2025directions}'s extension of one-dimensional truth directions to multi-dimensional cones in decoder LLMs: the verbal probability "axis" is more accurately a low-dimensional subspace whose syntactic-frame instantiations are partially redundant projections of a shared latent epistemic dimension, and larger models develop a more unified probability representation that becomes progressively less entangled with syntactic frame. The §4.4 ΔMAE-cos correlation ($r = 0.92$ on mxbai-embed-large, $r = 0.86$ on qwen3-embedding) is the functional confirmation of this subspace structure: the larger the geometric alignment between two within-type axes, the more downstream calibration is lost when one is erased from the other's embeddings. The §4.7 ensemble result reaches the same conclusion from the opposite direction. If the four axes were independent measurements of a shared latent quantity, ensembling them would average out independent noise and improve novel-phrase generalization; instead, no ensemble strategy beats the modal axis alone, and the gap to oracle is small (1–4 percentage points across models). At the dimensionalities tested the four axes have not collapsed into one, but they are organized as partially redundant projections of one underlying epistemic dimension whose modal-frame instantiation happens to dominate the syntactic distribution of natural hedging.

A useful way to read the empirical content of this paper is as evidence that distributional embedding geometry and human probability semantics are convergent measurements of one underlying quantity. Three independently collected psychometric datasets spanning more than fifty years \citep{mosteller1990quantifying,vogel2022systematic,wintle2019verbal}, six pretrained embedding models with different architectures and training procedures, and eight typologically diverse languages all agree on the same continuous probability axis, with cross-dataset $\rho$ reaching 0.991 (Mosteller$\to$Vogel) and 0.967 (Mosteller$\to$Wintle), cross-linguistic mean ranking $|\rho| = 0.928$, and concept-erasure functional confirmation across two architectures. Each instrument is exposed to a different source of measurement noise — Mosteller's 1990 sample of 238 science writers, Vogel's meta-analytic aggregation across 21 studies, Wintle's 2019 expert-elicitation protocol on $n \approx 924$, and the architectural and training-data heterogeneity of the embedding models — and their joint agreement on a common ordering and a common calibration is precisely what one would expect if each were a noisy measurement of a single latent quantity: the communicative probability function that human languages have evolved to express. This perspective, complementary to the standard "embeddings reflect their training data" framing, is supplied with measurement-theoretic scaffolding by \citet{schockaert2022formal}'s formal framework for embeddings as epistemic states. The §4.7 IQR negative further sharpens the picture. The geometry encodes a single stable point per expression rather than the spread of human interpretations, which is consistent with treating "probably," "possibly," and their kin as continuous-valued operators on a single probability dimension in the sense of \citet{lassiter2017graded}, and inconsistent with treating them as distributional objects encoding rater variance. The verbal probability axis is, in this sense, a *point estimator* on the population probability mapping — an object that recovers the central tendency of human verbal-probability semantics without recovering the dispersion. We submit this paper as one step in establishing the convergent-instruments frame empirically; the broader question of how far the frame extends to other graded human-meaningful concepts is the larger program it suggests.

A specific class of applications motivates further attention. Pretrained pooled sentence embedding models are the substrate of contemporary language-constituted agents — chatbots, scaffolded LLM systems, and the broader class of systems whose primary observation and action channels are linguistic. For such agents, reading off expressed uncertainty in a calibrated form is operationally load-bearing: any agentic protocol that depends on the agent's stated confidence — honest-activation discipline, calibration audits, theory-of-mind reasoning over an interlocutor's epistemic state — requires the linguistic encoding of uncertainty to map to numerical probability in a known way. The verbal probability axis recovered in this paper supplies one component of that measurement infrastructure: a way to read the uncertainty an agent expresses through language, calibrated against six decades of human psychometric replication, validated across architectures and languages, and confirmed as functional rather than merely correlated. \citet{schockaert2022formal}'s formal framework for embeddings as epistemic states supplies the conceptual scaffolding under which this reading is well-typed; the present paper supplies the empirical instrument. The strongest near-term applications we anticipate are in **honest-activation discipline** for scaffolded language-constituted agents (does an agent's expressed uncertainty match its model-internal state?), in **cross-substrate epistemic-state comparison** (do agents on different model classes encode the same uncertainty when shown the same proposition?), and in **detection of distributional drift** between expressed and actual confidence. We treat these as the most direct downstream uses of the present work and reserve their development for follow-up.

## 6. Limitations

We name eight limitations the present study does not resolve. Each is described in enough detail for a reader to assess what it would take to address; where appropriate we frame remediation as scope-of-future-work rather than as a flaw in the present claims. The first three are evaluation-coverage limitations (template scope, cross-linguistic ground truth, novel-phrase ground truth); the next is a control-set transparency point that we owe a skeptical reader; the final four concern the methodological scope of the §4.4 functional-validation experiment and the small psychometric calibration sets the field has inherited from \citet{mosteller1990quantifying}.

**(L1) Template-only evaluation.** Every Mosteller, Vogel, and Wintle result reported in §4.1–§4.2, the §4.3 cross-linguistic transfer, and the §4.4 concept-erasure experiment uses three controlled syntactic templates ("It is {X} that the experiment will succeed", "The experiment will {X} succeed", "There is a {X} that the experiment will succeed"). External validity to natural text is therefore an inferential step the present paper does not make rigorously. Section 4.5 partially mitigates this concern by showing that the trained axes rank novel non-template phrasings ("I think," "I doubt," "signs point to") with $\rho = 0.75$–$0.94$ across five models, and FINDINGS-02 documents a systematic projection-attenuation effect — natural sentences spread the difference vector across more dimensions than templates do, compressing high-probability magnitudes toward the center while preserving rank order. A corpus study with hedged claims drawn from scientific writing, weather forecasts, or IPCC assessment reports — domains where verbal probability and numerical probability are paired by editorial convention — would convert the ranking-preserved claim into a calibration claim on natural text. We have not run that study; the present paper claims template-controlled calibration and natural-text rank ordering, no more. Real-text calibration is reserved for follow-up work.

**(L2) Cross-linguistic results are ranking transfer, not native-speaker calibration.** The eight-language probability values used in §4.3 are constructed translations of English Mosteller equivalents — for example, Japanese ありそうな is paired with Mosteller's "likely" $\to$ 71.1% — not values elicited from native-speaker psychometric surveys in each target language. The ranking correlation we report (mean $|\rho| = 0.928$, with Korean predicative $\rho = 1.000$ and Hindi modal $\rho = 1.000$) is therefore a defensible claim: the English-trained axis ranks hedge expressions correctly within each language. The MAE figures (Japanese modal MAE $= 18.1\%$, Arabic modal MAE $= 3.6\%$) are not directly comparable across languages because the "expected" value column is itself a translation artifact. The Japanese modal results illustrate the gap most clearly: まさか projects at 53.5% under our English-derived calibration but is intuited by native speakers as conveying near-zero probability with strong counterfactual presupposition (its expected value of 5% in the table is itself a translation guess), and pragmatic-loaded Japanese epistemic markers more generally carry contextual implicatures that a template-driven evaluation cannot capture. Native-speaker psychometric validation in the eight target languages — replicating Mosteller's 1990 protocol with locally recruited raters — is the natural next step and is reserved for follow-up work; until then, §4.3 is honest only as a ranking-transfer claim, and we say so explicitly in the abstract and §1 contribution list.

**(L3) Author-generated probability ratings on novel phrases.** Section 4.5's novel-phrase generalization result ($\rho = 0.75$–$0.94$ across five models on 26 phrases not in Mosteller) uses author-rated "intuition" probabilities as the comparison target rather than independent psychometric ratings, and FINDINGS-02 acknowledges this explicitly. The phrases include informal hedges ("I think," "I believe," "I suspect"), doubt expressions ("I doubt," "I wonder if"), anti-hedges ("obviously," "without question"), and constructions outside Mosteller's vocabulary ("signs point to," "I'd wager," "it stands to reason"). Author intuition is sufficient to demonstrate that the axis does *something* sensible on out-of-vocabulary phrases — it does not collapse, the rank order tracks intended meaning, and the cross-axis consistency is high — but it is not sufficient to claim a calibrated psychometric mapping for those phrases. A small Prolific re-rating study ($n \geq 10$ annotators per phrase, on the order of US\$50 and one day of effort) would convert this into an independent-ground-truth result; the experimental design is identical to Mosteller's at much smaller scale. We have scoped this task as a revision-pass deliverable rather than a blocker for first submission, and treat the §4.5 numbers as evidence of generalization rather than as calibrated-prediction claims.

**(L4) The mixed non-epistemic adjective control shows residual structure that a skeptical reviewer will quote.** Section 4.6 reports two non-epistemic adjective control sets placed in the same predicative template. The pure set — "expensive," "complicated," "old," "new," "fast," "difficult," and similar items with no epistemic content — yields LOO $\rho = 0.31$ against author-assigned arbitrary labels, comfortably within the random-label null distribution and confirming that the method is not prone to template-structure overfitting. The mixed set, by contrast, returns LOO $\rho = 0.69$ (with in-sample $\rho = 0.94$) and triggers the warning line "WARNING: Non-epistemic adjectives show some structure" in the raw output of `experiment_10_null_hypothesis.py`. We retain that warning line rather than suppressing it, and explain its source: the mixed set was constructed to include adjectives at the boundary of epistemic content — "expected" (rated 70 on our arbitrary scale, but compositionally near-synonymous with "probable"), "obvious" (rated 90, compositionally near-synonymous with "certain"), "documented," "acknowledged," "well-known," and "unprecedented" all carry residual epistemic loading even though they are not on Mosteller's list. The script's source comments flag two of these adjectives as known-leakage cases at construction time. The honest reading is that the mixed-set $\rho = 0.69$ is *evidence for* the geometry's epistemic sensitivity — the axis picks up the residual epistemic content in adjectives like "expected" and "obvious" — rather than evidence against it. The pure set is the load-bearing comparison and remains clean. We report both results because hiding the WARNING line would invite exactly the reviewer objection we are trying to forestall, and because the contrast between the two sets is itself informative about what the axis is sensitive to.

**(L5) No causal intervention in the decoder-LLM steering sense.** The concept-erasure experiment in §4.4 establishes that the verbal probability axis is functional in the linear-decoder sense — rank-1 projection erasure raises cross-type calibration MAE in proportion to inter-axis cosine alignment (Pearson $r = 0.92 / 0.86$ across two models), and the headline predicative $\leftrightarrow$ modal pair clears $z_{\text{match\_MAE}} > +2$ against a cosine-matched random-direction control on both models in both directions. This is the embedding-class analogue of the activation-steering and representation-patching experiments standard in the decoder-LLM linear-feature literature \citep{ji2025calibrating,lepori2026fantasy,marks2023geometry,valois2025frame}; pretrained pooled sentence embedding models lack a token-generation pathway through which to verify that an intervention changes downstream model output, so representation-level erasure validated by a calibrated downstream linear decoder is the appropriate analogue rather than a substitute. We are explicit in §1, §4.4, and §5 that this is a model-class-analogue argument, not a claim of equivalence. We note also that \citet{lepori2026fantasy} include preliminary causal-steering evidence in Appendix C of their published paper, which strengthens rather than weakens the analogy: even decoder-class modal-categorization work treats causal steering as the validation method, and our concept-erasure design plays the same structural role in the embedding class. Bridging sentence-embedding probes to decoder-LLM behavior — for example, asking whether the verbal-uncertainty feature \citet{ji2025calibrating} localize in residual streams corresponds, under shared input, to the projection of the same sentence onto our pooled-embedding probability axis — is a natural follow-up question the present paper opens but does not answer.

**(L6) The cosine-matched random-direction control is over-conservative at high cosine alignment by construction.** The matched-random control direction in §4.4 is constructed as $\mathbf{r} = \pm \cos(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A, \hat{\mathbf{w}}_B) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{w}}_B + \sqrt{1 - \cos^2} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\perp}$, so it shares the $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_B$-aligned compression term with the real-axis erasure target by design — that is what makes it a stringent comparison. When $\cos(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_A, \hat{\mathbf{w}}_B)$ is high (predicative–modal at 0.88 on mxbai-embed-large), the matched-random direction is close to $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_B$ itself plus a small orthogonal residual, and it reproduces approximately 98% of the real-axis MAE damage by geometric construction; the remaining headroom for a real-axis effect to clear $z = +2$ is correspondingly compressed. The headline predicative $\leftrightarrow$ modal pair clears this stringent threshold on both models in both directions ($z_{\text{match\_MAE}}$ ranging from $+2.02$ to $+26.88$), but the symmetric concern is that at low cosine alignment (e.g., NounPhrase $\to$ Modal at $\cos = 0.34$), the matched-random direction is only $\sim 34\%$ $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_B$ plus 66% orthogonal noise — significantly more concentrated than a uniform-random unit vector in 1024-dimensional space, which inflates the matched-random MAE baseline and raises the bar for real-axis erasure to demonstrably exceed it. We treat the headline pair, along with the cross-pair Pearson $r(\cos, \Delta\text{MAE})$ of $+0.92 / +0.86$ across all twelve ordered pairs, as the load-bearing functional-validation result, and acknowledge per-pair functional verdicts on individual low-alignment pairs as below the experiment's resolving power. A control with the orthogonal residual sampled from the empirical embedding manifold rather than from isotropic Gaussian noise (one of the v3 design upgrades flagged in the open-dependencies notes) would address this concern more directly; we treat it as a robustness check available on reviewer request.

**(L7) Small per-type sample sizes saturate $\rho$-based metrics.** Within-type axis training uses Mosteller-derived sets of $n = 10$ (modal), 11 (noun phrase), 13 (predicative), and 19 (frequency adverb), and in-sample fit reaches $\rho \geq 0.95$ on every type for both mxbai-embed-large and qwen3-embedding. Rank-based metrics have essentially no headroom to register a functional erasure effect at these sample sizes: small projection shifts can flip neighboring ranks in either direction, and a rank-1 erasure that compresses magnitudes while preserving order leaves Spearman $\rho$ insensitive. This is not a hypothetical concern but the empirical situation in §4.4: the $\rho$-based view of the concept-erasure results yields 5/12 functional pairs on mxbai vs. 3/12 on qwen3 with zero overlap in which pairs flag as functional, which initially reads as cross-model disagreement; the MAE-based view, computed against the same cosine-matched null, recovers cross-model agreement (9/12 ordered pairs MAE-functional on both models simultaneously) and the unified Pearson $r(\cos, \Delta\text{MAE})$ signature. We treat $\rho$-saturation at small $n$ as a property of the available calibration data — Mosteller's 53 expressions remain the field standard six decades into this literature — rather than as a flaw in our extraction method, and we lead the §4.4 analysis with MAE precisely because it has the headroom that $\rho$ does not. A larger psychometric calibration corpus, with native-speaker validation and at least one additional decade of items per syntactic type, would tighten this margin and would also alleviate L8.

**(L8) The modal axis trains on $n = 10$ Mosteller-derived adverbial values.** Modal training is restricted to the ten adverbial expressions for which \citet{mosteller1990quantifying} report a median (e.g., "certainly," "probably," "possibly"). Earlier exploratory work used a larger modal set that included author-estimated values for "perhaps," "conceivably," "arguably," and similar items not in Mosteller's calibration; we excluded those values from the published axis to keep modal calibration psychometrically grounded, at the cost of the smallest training set of the four within-type axes. The modal axis is also the one most exposed to the L7 saturation concern, and qwen3-embedding's small $\rho$ instability on cross-type erasure pairs involving modal in §4.4 is consistent with this — the $n = 10$ training set leaves limited margin against rank-flipping under projection. Two remediations are available and neither is in scope for first submission. The first is to commission a small native-speaker psychometric study extending Mosteller's modal subset to $\sim 20$ expressions; this would also support the L2 cross-linguistic remediation if conducted in multiple languages. The second is to retain the seven excluded estimated-value modal terms with explicit "noisy" flags in the regression, accepting some calibration noise in exchange for sample-size headroom (a v3 design upgrade flagged in the open-dependencies notes). We treat both as future work and report the §4.4 modal results with the $n = 10$ caveat made explicit.

A final note on a near-miss in the related-work positioning that bears on §6 rather than §2. \citet{ji2025calibrating} include in their Appendix E.2 a cross-check of their decoder verbal-uncertainty feature against prototypical-uncertainty *sentence-embedding cosines*, drawing a small bridge between their decoder-internal feature and the pooled-sentence-embedding object we study. Their use of sentence-embedding cosine is a sanity check on a decoder feature rather than the primary geometric object of analysis — the calibration target, the model class, and the intervention method are decoder-internal throughout — but the appendix establishes that the two model classes are not as separated as a strict reading of §2.1 might suggest. We position our work as the embedding-class analogue rather than as wholly separate territory, and acknowledge that Ji et al.'s appendix is the closest single point of methodological contact between the decoder-internal linear-feature literature and the present paper.

## 7. Conclusion

We have shown that pretrained pooled sentence embedding models encode a continuously calibrated verbal probability axis aligned with human psychometric data: a single linear direction recovered within syntactic types from frozen pooled representations, calibrated against \citet{mosteller1990quantifying}, cross-validated against \citet{vogel2022systematic} and \citet{wintle2019verbal}, transferred zero-shot in ranking to eight typologically diverse languages, robust to dimensional truncation, and confirmed as functional rather than merely correlated by representation-level concept erasure under a cosine-matched random-direction control. The result holds across six pretrained embedding models spanning five architectural families and survives three null-hypothesis controls. It establishes for the pretrained pooled sentence embedding class a phenomenon the recent linear-feature interpretability literature has documented for decoder LLM internal states, with representation-level concept erasure standing in for token-level causal intervention and human psychometric data standing in for model-internal calibration targets.

Four directions extend the present work most directly. The first is native-speaker psychometric replication of the Mosteller protocol in the eight target languages of §4.3, which would convert the cross-linguistic ranking-transfer claim into a calibration-transfer claim and resolve the translation-pairing ambiguity §6 L2 names for languages such as Japanese where pragmatic-loaded epistemic markers diverge from English equivalents. The second is real-text corpus evaluation: hedged claims drawn from scientific writing, weather forecasts, and IPCC assessment reports — domains in which verbal probability and numerical probability are paired by editorial convention — would convert the template-controlled calibration of §4.1–§4.2 into a calibration claim on natural hedged text and address the external-validity concern of §6 L1. The third is bridging pretrained pooled sentence embedding probes to decoder-LLM internal-state probes by asking whether the verbal-uncertainty feature \citet{ji2025calibrating} localize in residual streams corresponds, under shared input, to the projection of the same sentence onto the pooled-embedding probability axis we recover here; \citeauthor{ji2025calibrating}'s Appendix E.2 already establishes a small bridge of this kind, and a systematic comparison would test whether the two model classes converge on the same epistemic dimension in the sense developed in §5 (cf. §6 L5). The fourth is enlarging the underlying psychometric calibration corpus — extending Mosteller's modal subset with native-speaker-validated supplements would alleviate the small-sample saturation §6 L7 and L8 jointly impose on the modal axis. We close on the framing §5 develops: distributional embedding geometry and human psychometric surveys are most usefully read as convergent instruments measuring one underlying quantity, and the present paper is one empirical step toward that frame.
